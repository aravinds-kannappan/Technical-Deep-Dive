    The same engineering question in two domains

    A clinical note and a sports event feed seem far apart. Yet both applications transform source evidence into language for a particular reader. Their difficult failures often occur where the meaning of that evidence changes: a negated symptom becomes a diagnosis, a missing score becomes zero, or a final play disappears before the writer sees it.

    This combined edition follows Synthure first and Replays second. It retains the full mathematical explanations and code excerpts from the standalone articles so the comparison rests on actual mechanisms. The closing section draws out the shared design decisions.

    The intended reader knows basic programming and wants to understand how models fit into a working application. Equations are introduced alongside the question they answer. Reported measurements remain tied to their source datasets; proposed changes are distinguished from implemented behavior.

    Part I · Synthure

    A clinical note can support several useful reports. The difficult part is keeping its meaning intact as the text becomes entities, codes, scores, and generated explanations.

    The conversations that led to Synthure

    While volunteering at a hospital, I asked people about their pain points. Three themes came up: patients did not always understand their care; administrative work suffered from poor communication; and people struggled to find the right provider and understand whether their coverage and spending fit their needs.

    These were connected problems. An explanation that a patient could not understand could become another question for the care team. Missing information could become another administrative handoff. Choosing a provider without understanding coverage and cost could leave someone with a decision that looked reasonable medically but made little sense financially.

    That was the motivation for Synthure. The question was broader than whether AI could summarize a clinical note. Could the information from an encounter become something useful to every person who needed to act on it, without making each person reconstruct the same story?

    The financial part of that question also goes beyond estimating a bill. A person wants care that fits their profile, a provider appropriate for their needs, and confidence that they are not paying more than necessary. A useful product has to connect clinical needs, coverage, provider suitability, and cost. A model that simply chooses the cheapest number would not solve that problem.

    The implementation examined here develops a foundation for that ambition: turn a messy note into a reviewable encounter record, connect that record to coding and reference data, and derive different views from the same state. Full personalized coverage and provider matching require additional data and validation; they are part of the larger product direction, rather than a capability established by the current code.

    This article starts with those hospital conversations and the product decisions they imply. The technical sections then follow the same questions: how to preserve what happened, how to identify what is missing, how to connect it to useful reference information, and how to keep every view consistent when someone makes a correction.

    Three needs, one underlying information problem

    Start with the patient. A note may be perfectly readable to a clinician and still fail as an explanation. The patient needs to understand what was found, which decisions remain open, what the next step is, and what information could change that step. Expanding abbreviations helps, but the more important task is preserving the distinction between a confirmed condition and a possibility.

    For example, “knee surgery is being considered” supports an explanation of a possible next step. It does not support instructions that assume surgery happened. This is why Synthure needs structured evidence before it needs polished language. The patient's experience depends on whether the software preserves status, uncertainty, and timing.

    Now consider the administrative team. A question such as “Is authorization on file?” has an owner, a state, and consequences for other people. If it is answered in one portal while another still shows it as unresolved, generating a better summary creates little value. The system needs a shared state transition that other views can observe.

    The provider-and-coverage problem is more demanding still. A diagnosis can help identify an appropriate kind of care, but it cannot determine plan eligibility, network participation, provider availability, a negotiated price, or whether a different option is a better fit. Those are separate inputs. Combining them responsibly requires explicit data joins and update policies, not asking a language model to infer missing insurance facts.

    What people neededWhat the product must representTechnical consequence

    This mapping determines the order of the engineering. First establish a reliable account of the encounter. Then connect the account to rules and reference data. Only then use it to support decisions and explanations. Beginning with a provider recommendation before those inputs exist would produce confidence faster than it produces knowledge.

    Why this is still hard when healthcare already has so much software

    There is no shortage of products for notes, coding, revenue-cycle work, or patient communication. Abridge describes encounter-grounded documentation and revenue-cycle capabilities, and Ambience exposes documentation and coding capabilities through APIs. These are examples of related offerings, not an exhaustive market comparison. Synthure's case cannot rest on a claim that nobody has attempted these problems.

    The product thesis is about continuity: preserve the meaning of the encounter as it moves between people, and let a verified change affect all the views that depend on it. That is a goal to demonstrate in a workflow, rather than an assertion of exclusive functionality.

    One obstacle is that each workflow asks a different question of the same note. A clinical phrase may be enough to explain a condition but insufficient to assign a specific code. A valid code may still leave missing documentation. Complete documentation does not establish coverage. Coverage does not establish that a particular provider is available or the best fit. These implications stop at different boundaries.

    Another obstacle is that the evidence changes at different rates. Clinical facts can be corrected during review. Plan benefits can change between coverage periods. Provider participation and availability need current sources. An explanation generated last week may be grammatically perfect while depending on an obsolete assumption.

    Access also differs by role. A shared internal representation does not imply that every reader should receive every field. The clinician's detailed view and an employer-facing aggregate view need different disclosure rules. Redacting an input is one useful operation; controlling what each downstream view can reveal is another.

    Finally, the feedback needed to learn each task is different. Mention-to-code examples can train a linker. They do not train a provider matcher or establish savings. A readiness label does not reveal whether a payer later accepted a claim. A company has to earn each of these claims with the appropriate data and outcome measurement.

    This explains the initial scope of Synthure: clinical-note normalization and a claim-readiness copilot. It is a concrete place to make the shared-information idea useful and testable. The broader matching ambition can build on that foundation when eligibility, provider, price, and outcome data are available.

    The first product decision: make the encounter the unit of work

    Before following the implementation, separate an ordinary rule from a learned model. A rule is written directly, such as “do not accept a code absent from the catalog.” A model is a calculation whose adjustable numbers were fitted using examples. Training chooses those numbers; inference uses the fixed numbers on a new note. Neither operation supplies facts absent from the input.

    The browser runs on the user's device. The server is the remote application coordinating requests and other services. An API specifies the request and response formats they use to communicate. We will follow the information across these boundaries rather than treat “AI” as one operation.

    Four separate AI summaries would reproduce the fragmentation that motivated the company. Each could reinterpret the same phrase, choose different details, and become stale after a correction. Synthure instead builds a shared record and uses it to support patient, physician, hospital, and employer views.

    The record begins with the note, but grows beyond text. An entity is a meaningful item found in the text, such as a condition, medication, or identifier. The record holds these items alongside possible medical codes, missing details, proposed services, and reference prices. Shared state means the current accepted version of this information, which several screens can read.

    The browser runs local redaction and biomedical extraction. The server adds lookups, compact-model predictions, and hosted narration. These are different kinds of computation. Extraction proposes what the note says; lookup connects it to a catalog; prediction estimates a labeled outcome; narration explains the resulting record.

    note → cleaned text → supported mentions → candidate codes and gaps
    → reviewable encounter → derived workflow state → audience-specific explanation

    That division is a response to the original pain points. Local processing limits raw-note transmission in the normal browser workflow. Constrained catalog selection narrows coding errors. A shared encounter makes a correction reusable. Audience-specific writers help with comprehension after the evidence has been assembled.

    The architecture creates a useful debugging question: where did meaning change? If every view incorrectly marks a proposed procedure as completed, inspect the common record. If only the patient explanation changes its status, inspect the writer. If a reviewed correction fails to reach another portal, inspect state propagation. The product problem gives each technical boundary a purpose.

    From an attractive prediction to a defensible task

    The repository records a revealing change in direction. An earlier version used note keywords to produce a denial-risk score. The README explains that this score was removed. The implemented direction separates documentation readiness and explicit checks from an unsupported probability of denial.

    Why does that matter? Predicting a denial would require a target such as the eventual outcome of a submitted claim, linked to the information available when the prediction was made. A model trained on generated documentation labels learns a different task. Calling its output a denial probability would not change the supervision it received.

    Readiness is narrower and more actionable. Is a required detail missing? Is the code valid? Is an authorization requirement unresolved in the record? These questions can lead to a specific review task. A clinician or coder can inspect the reason and supply the missing information.

    The current system contains both a learned documentation-readiness predictor and deterministic readiness checks. They should remain distinguishable. The predictor summarizes patterns in its training label; the checks expose individually inspectable conditions. Neither certifies coverage or establishes the outcome of an external claim.

    This evolution connects the company motivation to model design. The ambition is to reduce confusion and unnecessary work. A score contributes only if its meaning is clear enough for someone to use and challenge. Otherwise it creates another opaque artifact that staff must interpret.

    Following one note through the system

    We will use this note throughout: “Patient A has type 2 diabetes and right-knee osteoarthritis. No chest pain. Knee surgery is being considered. Medication details are incomplete; prior authorization status is unknown.”

    There are two positive conditions, a negated symptom, a possible future procedure, and unresolved documentation. Flattening this into a list of medical phrases would lose most of the distinctions that matter.

    The browser first loads its models, runs deidentify(note), and then runs extractEntities(deid.text). A model-loading or inference failure stops the normal browser path before the synthesis POST. Extracting after redaction matters because replacing a name with a placeholder can move every later character. Entity offsets must describe the same cleaned string the server receives.

    From token predictions to clinical assertions

    The first task is to find meaningful phrases without changing what they say. In “Alex reports right knee pain,” the system might locate a name and a condition. The name can be replaced before the note is sent onward; the condition becomes an item for review. Named-entity recognition, or NER, assigns these categories to pieces of text.

    A span is a continuous stretch of characters. A token is a piece used by the model: a word, part of a word, or punctuation. The model labels tokens, but the application needs spans that a person can highlight and verify.

    Try a threshold before looking inside the model. In this invented example, “Alex” receives a name score of 0.94 and “right knee pain” a condition score of 0.86. There is also an error: “reports” receives a condition score of 0.55. A threshold is the minimum score accepted. Raise it above 0.55 to remove the error; raise it above 0.86 and a useful condition disappears too.

    The slider changes an acceptance rule, not the model. A score of 0.86 does not automatically mean an 86% chance that the clinical assertion is correct. That interpretation would need calibration on suitable labeled examples. Here, inspect what the rule keeps and what it discards.

    What the entity model does with a sentence

    Now consider No chest pain. Right knee pain persists. A tokenizer converts text pieces into vocabulary IDs, like recording dictionary entry numbers. Those IDs are lookup keys: token 200 does not mean twice as much as token 100.

    Each ID becomes an ordered list of numbers. Such a list is a vector; a learned numerical representation is an embedding. The entries need not represent concepts we can name individually. Their purpose is to support useful transformations and comparisons. A transformer is a model architecture that lets a token's representation depend on other available tokens, so “pain” near “No” can acquire a different representation from “pain” near “persists.”

    A final classification head converts the contextual vector into a score for each allowed label. These unbounded scores are logits. Softmax turns them into positive shares adding up to one. Exponentiating means raising the constant e (approximately 2.718) to each score. For toy logits 2 and 1, e²≈7.39 and e¹≈2.72. Dividing each by their sum, 10.11, gives shares 0.731 and 0.269. This makes a larger logit receive a larger share.

    Optional notation: the token classifier

    i names a token position; hᵢ is its vector; d is the number of vector entries. W is a table of learned weights and b a list of starting scores. Their multiplication and addition produce label logits zᵢ. There are L possible labels. ℝ means real-valued numbers, ∈ means “belongs to,” and Σ means “add these terms.” The last expression is the exponentiate-and-divide softmax calculation above. Its pᵢ,ℓ is the share for label ℓ at token position i; the index j runs over all labels.

    hᵢ ∈ ℝᵈ → zᵢ = Whᵢ + b ∈ ℝᴸ → pᵢ,ℓ = exp(zᵢ,ℓ) / Σⱼ exp(zᵢ,ⱼ)

    B-DISEASE means “begin a disease mention”; I-DISEASE means “continue that mention”; O means “outside the recognized categories.” These labels locate text. They do not by themselves say whether a condition is present, absent, historical, or uncertain.

    The application still has work after the model returns labels. It must merge compatible pieces, locate the character range in the cleaned string, and decide whether the span clears the threshold. If the three pieces of a mention have scores 0.96, 0.87, and 0.92, the inspected minimum-score aggregation returns 0.87. It neither multiplies the scores nor estimates the probability that the complete clinical assertion is correct. The predictions share context and should not be assumed independent.

    Most importantly, locating “chest pain” is different from asserting that the patient has it. An extractor can correctly highlight both pain mentions while a downstream system incorrectly treats both as present. A useful record needs the mention, its source range, and its assertion status. That separation explains why patient comprehension and administrative accuracy depend on more than a high entity-detection score.

    What int8 changes, and what it leaves intact

    Quantization stores some model numbers with fewer bits, rather like recording a measurement to fewer decimal places. With step size 0.01, weight 0.123 becomes 0.123/0.01=12.3, rounds to integer 12, and reconstructs as 12×0.01=0.12. Storage is smaller, but the reconstructed value is slightly different.

    A general version includes a zero point, an integer representing real zero. It writes q=clamp(round(w/s)+z₀) and reconstructs ŵ=s(q−z₀). Here w is the original weight, s the step size, z₀ the zero point, q the saved integer, and ŵ the reconstructed weight. “Clamp” keeps an integer within the supported range. This explains the idea rather than specifying every operation in the exported models.

    One million int8 weight entries occupy approximately one megabyte of payload, compared with four megabytes for float32 entries. Scale metadata, intermediate activations, downloads, and runtime buffers add other costs. The browser's int8 models therefore reduce a particular storage component; they do not imply a fourfold end-to-end speedup or establish identical extraction quality.

    BIO tags mark the beginning and continuation of an entity. The aggregation code joins compatible pieces, removes WordPiece’s ## marker, and searches forward for the reconstructed phrase. It assigns the minimum token score to a merged span. This conservative aggregation prevents a weak token from being hidden by an average, although it is not a calibrated probability of correct clinical interpretation.

    A span is saved using character positions. In [start,end), the start is included and the end excluded: [5,10) covers five characters, positions 5 through 9. Replacing those five characters with twelve moves every later character seven places. Replacing the last span first avoids moving any earlier span that still needs editing.

    Why some decisions fit small models

    Identifying a note category or checking for missing documentation is a narrower task than writing a patient explanation. Synthure serves these decisions using exported weights in TypeScript. This avoids a hosted generation call and makes the computation inspectable.

    Build the feature vector by hand

    We need numbers before a small classifier can score a note. A feature is a measurable property used in that calculation. Here the features are counts of terms. Use a tiny vocabulary: pain, knee, and knee pain. A single word is a unigram; an adjacent pair is a bigram. “Knee pain; pain persists” has counts [2,1,1]. This ordered list is the feature vector, and its positions must keep the same meaning.

    TF-IDF combines term frequency—how often a term occurs in this note—with inverse document frequency—how rare it was across the training documents. Assume saved IDF weights [1.0,1.5,2.0]. These are illustrative values; inference loads the trained weights rather than fitting new ones from this note.

    First soften repeated occurrences with 1+ln(count). The natural logarithm, written ln, grows more slowly than the count: ln(1)=0 and ln(2)≈0.693. Two appearances therefore receive weight 1.693 before IDF rather than twice the weight of one. Multiply by each feature's own IDF to obtain [1.693,1.500,2.000].

    Next put the vector on a common scale. Square each entry, add the squares, and take the square root. This gives its L2 length, about 3.019. Divide every entry by this same length to get [0.561,0.497,0.662]. This normalization preserves feature proportions while reducing the effect of overall magnitude. We call the unnormalized vector v and the normalized vector x. The symbol ‖v‖₂ below means exactly this length.

    counts = [2, 1, 1]
    v = [(1+ln 2)×1, (1+ln 1)×1.5, (1+ln 1)×2]
    v ≈ [1.693, 1.500, 2.000]
    ‖v‖₂ = √(1.693² + 1.500² + 2.000²) ≈ 3.019
    x = v/‖v‖₂ ≈ [0.561, 0.497, 0.662]

    A possible note category, or class, has one learned coefficient per feature. With toy coefficients [0.4,1.0,0.8], multiply corresponding entries: 0.4×0.561=0.224, 1.0×0.497=0.497, and 0.8×0.662=0.530. Adding gives about 1.251. Multiplying corresponding entries and adding them is called a dot product.

    The class also has an intercept, a starting score before the feature contributions. An intercept of −0.3 gives −0.3+1.251≈0.951. This is a logit, not yet a probability. Another class has different weights and an intercept, so the same input produces another logit.

    This is why “the model predicts a note type” can be made inspectable: each multiplication contributes an amount to the final logit. The chart below plots those amounts for two toy classes. It uses a two-class softmax for clarity; the real classifier uses its exported class set.

    A useful debugging sequence is now explicit: inspect token counts, then vocabulary indices, then IDF-weighted values, then the normalized vector, then each class logit. Checking only the final label can hide an indexing error or a preprocessing difference between Python training and TypeScript serving.

    Optional notation: dot products and stable softmax

    x is the feature vector; j names a class; wⱼ contains its weights; bⱼ is its intercept. Superscript T turns a column into a row so that wⱼᵀx denotes the dot product. zⱼ is the logit and pⱼ its softmax share. The index k runs over all classes. “max z” is the largest logit. Subtracting it keeps exponentials numerically manageable without changing the shares.

    zj = bj + wjTx
    pj = exp(zj − max z) / Σk exp(zk − max z)

    The subtraction matters because exponentials grow quickly. For logits 1,002 and 1,001, subtracting the largest produces 0 and −1. The relative odds remain the same while intermediate numbers become small enough to represent reliably.

    Missing fields: several labels can be true

    Missing-field detection uses another list of features: counts, yes/no indicators, note length, and note type. A yes/no indicator is 1 or 0. A one-hot note-type encoding has one position per possible category and sets only the selected category's position to 1.

    Each missing-field question receives its own logit. Sigmoid maps one logit to a value between zero and one: σ(z)=1/(1+e^(−z)). Here z is the logit, e the constant approximately 2.718, and σ the function's name. Logit 0 maps to 0.5, logit 1 to about 0.731, and −1 to about 0.269. Separate sigmoids do not have to add up to one. Dosage and laterality can both be missing, so these labels should not be forced to compete.

    The returned present flag means the missing-field prediction exceeded 0.5. It does not mean the medical detail was supplied. There is also a representational limit: “Left arm examined; knee pain” contains a side, but not the knee’s side. Numerical parity with training cannot repair a feature representation that loses this association.

    Inference is the execution of a learned decision rule

    Training chooses the vocabulary, IDF values, coefficients, and intercepts from examples. Inference holds those artifacts fixed and calculates one new note's scores. “Exported weights” means the learned numbers were saved in a format the TypeScript application can read, allowing it to execute the calculation without asking a hosted writer.

    With C classes and m nonzero features, scoring does roughly C×m multiply-and-add operations after tokenization. O(Cm) describes how work grows, not a measured response time. Skipping zero features is called sparse computation.

    That makes these models suitable for frequent, narrow decisions in the workflow. It also makes model versioning inseparable from preprocessing versioning. Deploying new coefficients with an old vocabulary changes the meaning of every multiplication. A useful export test compares intermediate feature vectors and logits between training and serving, before softmax or rounding can hide discrepancies.

    Retrieving a code is easier than establishing that it applies

    The next task connects a supported phrase to an entry in a medical-code catalog. Retrieval means finding plausible entries; ranking puts those candidates in order. Two code families appear here: ICD codes identify diagnoses, and CPT codes identify procedures or services. Their descriptions and detailed rules matter; they are more specific than free-form medical phrases. Restricting the output to catalog entries prevents invented identifiers, but a real identifier can still be wrong for the note. Abstention means declining to return a code when the available match is insufficient.

    Follow the inverted index through one lookup

    An inverted index reverses a catalog. Instead of asking each description whether it contains “knee,” it stores the list of descriptions containing “knee” once. Consider a toy catalog with three descriptions: A, “right knee osteoarthritis”; B, “left knee osteoarthritis”; and C, “right shoulder osteoarthritis.”

    TokenStored candidate IDs
    rightA, C
    kneeA, B
    osteoarthritisA, B, C

    Intersecting all three lists for “right knee osteoarthritis” leaves A. For “knee osteoarthritis,” intersection leaves A and B. No ranking score can recover a side that was never specified. Relaxing token requirements helps when the catalog uses different wording, but also admits candidates with conflicting qualifiers.

    The local reranker converts each surviving candidate into four features and computes a logistic score. It is a second decision over a small set, rather than a second search over the entire catalog. The acceptance floor then decides whether to return the best candidate at all. Retrieval, ranking, and abstention respectively answer “what is available?”, “which is best?”, and “is the evidence sufficient to use it?”

    Retrieving a code and retrieving evidence for a writer also serve different purposes. The first links a mention to a controlled vocabulary. Retrieval-augmented generation supplies selected source material inside a writer's context. Both can use vector search, but a good code match does not by itself supply a payer policy or prove that a proposed service is covered.

    Learning similarity when words do not overlap

    A word-based search can miss phrases that mean the same thing using different words. The optional neural coder turns both the note phrase and catalog descriptions into embeddings: learned lists of numbers intended to make useful matches numerically similar. Similarity is a comparison score, not a medical conclusion.

    From token vectors to a searchable catalog

    An encoder is a model that creates vectors. Suppose it returns four rows of three numbers. This rectangular table is a matrix with shape 4×3: one row per token position and three features per row. If the fourth row is padding used to make inputs equally long, a mask says to ignore it.

    To make one vector for the phrase, average each column over the real rows. With rows [1,0,1], [1,1,0], and [0,1,1], every column sums to 2. Divide by three real rows to get [2/3,2/3,2/3]. This is mean pooling. Divide that vector by its L2 length to get [0.577,0.577,0.577], a unit vector whose length is one.

    The shorthand is u=Σᵢmᵢhᵢ/Σᵢmᵢ. Here hᵢ is row i; mᵢ is 1 for a real token or 0 for padding; Σ means to add. The numerator adds retained rows, and the denominator counts them. It is the same column-by-column average just calculated.

    The catalog stores one such vector per description. With C codes and embedding width d, its matrix has shape C×d; multiplying it by a single query vector produces C similarity scores. This is the basic computation behind exhaustive dense retrieval. Approximate indexes change how candidates are found, but are not necessary to understand the score.

    Separately encoding each description makes caching possible. Jointly encoding a mention and description allows their tokens to interact before scoring, but that computation depends on the particular pair and cannot be fully precomputed for every future mention.

    Why contrastive loss changes the embedding space

    How does the encoder learn useful similarities? Give it a known matching phrase and description alongside competing descriptions. A loss measures how poorly it performs on this training task; training tries to lower it. In this contrastive task, the correct pair should receive a larger share than competitors.

    Work through two candidates with similarities 0.8 and 0.2, where the first is correct. Divide by a chosen temperature of 0.2 to get logits 4 and 1. Exponentiating gives approximately 54.60 and 2.72. The correct share is 54.60/(54.60+2.72)≈0.953. Applying −ln(share) gives loss about 0.049. Equal similarities would give share 0.5 and the larger loss 0.693.

    The negative logarithm makes a confidently missed positive expensive: a correct-pair share of 0.01 gives loss about 4.605. Temperature changes how sharply differences affect shares. It does not add evidence about whether a code applies. These shares describe competition within the batch, not calibrated clinical probabilities.

    A gradient describes how a small adjustment would change the loss. Gradient descent adjusts learned numbers in a direction that reduces the loss. In this example, the training signal pushes the positive similarity upward and competing similarities downward. Backpropagation carries that signal backward through the encoder to its adjustable weights.

    The competitors are called negatives, but two mentions may share the same correct code. Treating one valid A description as the other mention's wrong answer would punish a legitimate synonym.     The diagonal outlines mark the designated positive pairs. The duplicated A descriptions are valid matches even though only one occupies each diagonal position. Masking the other same-code column removes it from the competition. The displayed loss is the average row cross-entropy; the source additionally averages the reverse, description-to-mention direction. Both directions depend on the examples that share a batch.

    A bi-encoder encodes the mention and description separately. The application can therefore calculate catalog vectors in advance. For a new note, it creates the query vector and compares it with saved descriptions. For unit vectors, their dot product equals cosine similarity: 1 means the same direction, 0 perpendicular directions, and −1 opposite directions. These are directions in learned feature space, not physical directions.

    A cross-encoder reads the mention and candidate description together. Their words can interact before it assigns a score, but a new pair requires another model calculation. A common design therefore retrieves a broad set cheaply and then scores a small shortlist jointly.

    The training objective

    The implementation stores all comparisons in a square score table. Each row is one mention, each column one description, and designated matches occupy the diagonal.     Turning a model score into a review policy

    Our example still has incomplete medication details and an unknown authorization status. A readiness predictor can help prioritize review, but cannot turn those unknowns into facts. Its job is to estimate a labeled documentation outcome from the features available.

    Trace a tree before interpreting its probability

    A decision tree is a series of questions about an input. An illustrative first question asks whether diagnosis count is zero. The answer chooses a left or right branch, perhaps leading to another question. Eventually the calculation reaches a leaf, a stored numerical contribution. One note visits one leaf in each tree, rather than averaging all the possible leaves.

    Gradient boosting combines many trees. Training adds trees that improve the current predictions; inference follows their stored questions and adds the chosen leaf contributions. Positive contributions raise the logit, negative ones lower it. A learning-rate multiplier sets the size of each contribution. Sigmoid then converts the final logit into a score between zero and one.

    Calibration is a separate mapping that tries to make score values agree with observed frequencies. A model can rank easier cases above harder ones while being overconfident about both. Calibration changes that interpretation; it does not supply a missing fact.

    Suppose a raw score of 0.5 maps to calibrated 0.4. The intention is that comparable cases assigned about 0.4 meet the labeled outcome about 40% of the time. It is not a claim that this note is “40% complete.” Nor does the label describe payer approval unless that is actually the outcome used in training.

    The vertical line is a decision policy; the points are model outputs. Moving the line does not retrain the model. It changes which cases enter the accepted set. With a small sample, one case can sharply change the displayed error rate, so evaluation needs counts as well as percentages.     What the evaluations actually measure

    The repository’s evaluation ledger is most useful when read as a set of component experiments. The dataset and denominator are part of every result. Combining unlike tests into one impression of “system accuracy” would lose the information needed to improve the system.

    Top-k accuracy asks whether the correct answer appears in the first k results. Mean reciprocal rank also rewards earlier placement: ranks 1, 2, and 5 contribute 1, 0.5, and 0.2. A miss contributes zero under the usual convention. Retrieval coverage and ordering should be examined separately.

    For extraction, begin with human-labeled supported mentions. A true positive (TP) is a real mention the system finds. A false positive (FP) is an incorrect mention it adds. A false negative (FN) is a real mention it misses.

    Precision asks, “Of the returned mentions, how many were right?” Recall asks, “Of the real mentions, how many were found?” If twelve real mentions exist, eight are found, and two incorrect ones are added, TP=8, FP=2, and FN=4. Precision is 8/(8+2)=0.80; recall is 8/(8+4)≈0.667. Their denominators answer different questions.

    F1 combines the two with a harmonic mean: multiply precision and recall, double the result, and divide by their sum. Here 2×0.80×0.667/(0.80+0.667)≈0.727. It falls when either component is low. It does not express how serious a particular mistake is.

    precision = TP/(TP+FP)
    recall = TP/(TP+FN)
    F1 = 2 × precision × recall / (precision + recall)

    Add false positives while leaving true positives fixed: precision falls because more returned mentions are wrong. Add false negatives instead: recall falls because more real mentions were missed. Counts make the distinction easier to interpret than one overall “accuracy” number.

    Two other measures appear below. AUROC measures ordering across thresholds: roughly, whether a randomly chosen positive case receives a higher score than a negative case. It does not establish that 0.8 means an 80% chance. Expected calibration error (ECE) groups predictions into score ranges and averages gaps between confidence and observed outcomes, weighted by group size. Its value depends on the grouping and outcome definition; lower is generally better under the same setup.

    Reconstruct one event from arbitrary network chunks

    The network transports bytes, not application objects. Imagine the server writes data: {"type":"report"} . One read might end after "rep; the next contains ort"} . Parsing the first chunk immediately would fail even though the server sent valid data.

    The decoder turns bytes into characters, preserving an incomplete multibyte character until the next read. A separate text buffer—accumulated text held in memory—preserves an incomplete event. Only once the double newline arrives does the client extract a full frame and parse its JSON, the structured-text format used for the event.

    A loop handles several events arriving in one read: it removes complete frames one at a time and retains any unfinished remainder. The two buffers solve distinct problems, bytes into characters and characters into events. Neither should be skipped because one simple test happened to deliver one full event per read.

Rejecting A's event prevents an old report from overwriting B's display. Cancellation is an additional operation that attempts to stop A's remaining work.     Part II · Replays AI

    A sports recap is a small data pipeline with an editorial problem inside it: decide what matters, preserve the facts, and deliver the result before the fan loses interest.

    The product starts with the fan’s time

    A fan can care deeply about a team without having time to watch every game, inspect every box score, and keep up with every player. The useful product question is how much of that work a personal sports desk can take over while still giving the fan a faithful sense of what happened.

    Replays AI's documented product direction is a personalized desk built from real NBA and NFL data: a feed, narrated reels, a two-host broadcast, picks, and a weekly newsletter. Team and player preferences give the system an initial idea of what matters without requiring an account.

    These surfaces solve different parts of the same problem. The feed helps a fan notice relevant games. A recap explains one result. A short broadcast fits a limited listening window. A newsletter connects a week's events. The company needs a consistent account of sport that can adapt to the fan's interests and available time.

    That is more demanding than producing a fluent paragraph. A model can write a convincing story about the wrong game, select the spectacular play that did not affect the result, or make correct narration impossible to fit into the chosen clip. Product quality emerges from the whole path.

    We will follow an invented 104–101 Owls–Foxes game from provider records to ranked evidence, statistical analysis, token-by-token generation, and playback. The equations below distinguish implemented scoring rules, general model mechanics, and proposed improvements. That distinction matters because “the AI” is several different computations.

    Why a personal sports desk is more than automated highlights

    Automated sports content is an existing category. For example, WSC Sports describes automated video and personalized highlight experiences. Replays' opportunity is therefore a product hypothesis about the fan experience, not a claim to have invented automated highlights.

    The organizing idea is that preferences should carry across formats. A fan who follows one team and two players should not have to reconstruct those interests in a stats page, a broadcast, and a weekly digest. The same preference profile should help decide which evidence enters each experience, while the format determines how that evidence is presented.

    The constraints explain the architecture. Sports data changes quickly, so the application needs normalization and freshness rules. A short recap has limited room, so it needs evidence selection. Fans differ, so it needs a ranker. Narration is sequential, so it needs generation and timing budgets. Popular finished games attract repeated requests, so it needs reuse and concurrency control.

    It is useful to express the goal as maximizing useful, supported information within the fan's available time. This is a design objective, not an objective currently optimized by a single learned model:

    Choose content that is relevant and supported by evidence.
    Keep it within the fan’s time budget and the system’s delivery limits.

    Adding another model helps only if it improves one of those terms. More colorful commentary can lower quality if it adds unsupported tactical claims. More retrieved events can slow inference and bury the important moment. Longer broadcasts can explain more while failing the user's two-minute constraint.

    The company-level measurements would therefore include whether fans finish the selected experience, find it useful, return for their teams, and encounter factual errors. Latency and cost per useful delivered experience belong beside those outcomes. The repository does not establish measured product-market fit or those business results; it gives us the implementation with which to test the hypothesis.

    Four meanings of inference in one product

    A model is a rule with adjustable numbers that maps inputs to outputs. Training chooses those numbers using examples; inference uses the chosen numbers on a new input. Some Replays components instead use rules written directly by the developer. The distinction tells us whether a behavior came from data, a hand-chosen assumption, or a larger pretrained writer.

    A named agent can be a rule, a small classifier, or a language-model call. Treating them all as equivalent hides where the behavior comes from and how to evaluate it.

    A classifier chooses a category, such as a play type. A ranker orders items by a score. A feature is a measurable input, such as whether the fan follows a team. An adapter is a small learned addition that changes a base model’s behavior. The sections below unpack each computation; these names describe different jobs rather than levels of intelligence.

    Inference means applying the fixed computation to new inputs. Training means estimating its adjustable parameters from examples. A sigmoid can appear in either a hand-designed proxy or a trained logistic model; the presence of the same function does not give them the same provenance.

    The rest of the article follows the boundaries between these computations. Provider data establishes the facts. Selection decides what survives. Ranking decides what comes first for the fan. Generation decides how to say it. Each stage can succeed numerically while the product fails semantically, so each needs a target that matches its responsibility.

    A shared game record supports several products

    The frontend is the interface in the fan's browser. The backend is the server that obtains data, runs decisions, and returns results. They communicate through an API: named operations with agreed request and response formats. Requesting a recap means sending a game identifier to one of those operations.

    A fallback is a simpler alternate route used when a preferred route fails. A template, for example, can insert known team names and scores into fixed sentences. It may be less expressive but still useful. A generated artifact means a saved output such as a recap, broadcast, or newsletter; it is not a separate kind of model.

    Replays uses a React frontend and a FastAPI backend. The backend reads ESPN data on demand and converts it into consistent fields. Redis, when configured, supports shared storage for artifacts and fan state. The live request path should be distinguished from historical ingestion or PostgreSQL code elsewhere in the repository.

    Fan chooses teams and players
  → browser requests a feed or game
  → backend obtains ESPN data
  → adapter converts it into consistent fields

    The products diverge after this common beginning. A recap needs a coherent account of one game. A newsletter first chooses which games belong in a fan’s feed. A reel needs available video and narration that fits its scenes. A broadcast needs structured turns that a player can schedule.

    Keeping these responsibilities separate makes failures easier to diagnose. A wrong score in several products suggests an adapter or source issue. A correct score attached to an irrelevant newsletter suggests selection or ranking. Correct narration over the wrong clip suggests media alignment.

    Sharing facts also creates dependencies. A corrected game record can invalidate several generated artifacts. A preference change affects personalized selection without changing the game itself. A missing clip breaks one presentation while leaving a text recap usable. Each artifact therefore needs an identity and freshness policy appropriate to what it depends on.

    Fallbacks make the design practical. Known scores can still become deterministic prose when generation fails. Newsletter and broadcast routes can attempt a configured trained writer, a general model, and then a template. The successful route needs to remain visible: a training script in the repository does not establish that a particular response used its saved adapter.

    Caching is a decision about which result remains valid

    Generating a recap takes time and may require a paid model call. If another fan asks for exactly the same recap a moment later, repeating the entire job is wasteful. A cache is saved work that can be reused. Finding the saved answer is a cache hit; finding nothing usable is a cache miss.

    The saved answer needs a name, called its key. Replays uses a key such as recap:v2:123: “the version-2 recap for game 123.” The application first checks a small cache inside the running server process. It then checks Redis, a separate storage service that multiple server processes can share. A process is one running copy of the application; memory inside one copy is not automatically visible to another.

    Saved does not always mean current

    Suppose the cached record says the Owls have 50 points. They score again, but the saved record still says 50. The cache is now stale. A time to live, or TTL, limits how long a saved entry may be reused before it expires. Expiration is permission to refresh; it does not itself prove that the source has been checked.

    Why two misses can produce two model calls

    1. Request A checks the key. There is no saved recap, so A begins generating it.
    2. Before A finishes, request B checks the same key. There is still no saved recap, so B begins generating it too.

    What the play-analysis agents actually compute

    Before writing a story, Replays attaches short analyses to plays. The modules named Scout, Stat, Ref, and Predict are currently rules that inspect text and structured fields. For example, a rule may notice “touchdown” or calculate the score difference. They do not watch a video, and their names do not imply separately trained models.

    A heuristic is a deliberately chosen rule that approximates something useful. A proxy is a substitute measurement: it may track part of the phenomenon without measuring the whole thing. Replays' kick and win scores are mathematical proxies with hand-set constants. We can understand exactly how they behave before deciding how much trust to place in them.

    Start with the shape of a probability-like score

    A kick should generally become less likely to succeed as distance increases. The sigmoid function turns an unrestricted number into a smooth value between zero and one. Its middle is 0.5. Large positive inputs approach one; large negative inputs approach zero. Here “approach” means getting closer without jumping abruptly.

    The kick formula first compares the attempt with 53.5 yards, then scales the difference by 6.5. In the next equation, d is distance in yards, p is the resulting score, and exp(a) means e raised to power a, where e is approximately 2.718.

    p = 1 ÷ [1 + exp((d − 53.5) ÷ 6.5)]
    1. For a 45-yard attempt, subtract the reference distance: 45 − 53.5 = −8.5.
    2. Divide by the scale: −8.5 ÷ 6.5 ≈ −1.308.
    3. Exponentiate: exp(−1.308) ≈ 0.270.
    4. Add one and take the reciprocal: 1 ÷ 1.270 ≈ 0.787.

    At 53.5 yards, the difference is zero, exp(0)=1, and the score is 1/(1+1)=0.5. The constants therefore determine where the curve crosses its midpoint and how quickly it changes. They were not fitted to the current kicker or weather. A number between zero and one is not automatically a reliable real-world probability.

    Make a lead matter more as time runs out

    The win proxy starts with the lead, called the margin. It then makes the same lead more influential when little regulation time remains. A three-point lead halfway through a basketball game leaves plenty of time for the opponent; the same lead with thirty seconds left means something different.

    1. Divide remaining seconds by total regulation seconds to obtain the fraction of time left. Halfway through a game, this is 0.5.
    2. Use the square root of that fraction and multiply by seven. At halftime, the scale is about 4.950.
    3. Divide the margin by the scale: a three-point lead gives 3/4.950≈0.606.
    4. Apply the sigmoid, giving a score around 0.647. This final score differs from the 0.606 input.

    The code floors the time fraction at 0.02. Near the end, the scale is therefore about 0.990 and the three-point lead produces about 0.954. A floor prevents a quantity from falling below a specified minimum; here it stops the formula from becoming arbitrarily extreme as time reaches zero.

    The model ignores possession, timeouts, and team strength. Missing kick distance even returns zero, although “unknown” and “impossible” mean different things. These limitations suggest tests before larger models: absent distances, malformed clocks, overtime, and missed shots.     What happens inside the writer at inference time

    The writer has a different task from the ranker. The ranker chooses which facts matter; the writer turns the selected facts into a readable account. To understand the writer, follow one new piece of text through the model rather than beginning with the full transformer equation.

    1. Convert the prompt into numbers

    The prompt is the supplied context: instructions plus the selected fact sheet. A tokenizer splits that text into vocabulary pieces called tokens and assigns each piece an integer ID. A token can be a word, part of a word, punctuation, or another text fragment. Token IDs are lookup keys; ID 200 does not have twice the meaning of ID 100.

    An embedding is a learned list of numbers retrieved for an ID. It provides the model with a starting numerical representation of the token. A list is a vector; many vectors stacked into rows form a matrix, a rectangular table of numbers. If a prompt has 100 tokens and each representation has 64 numbers, the initial table has 100 rows and 64 columns. Those dimensions are a toy example, not a claim about the hosted model.

    The model also represents position so it can distinguish different orders. Layers then update these representations using the surrounding permitted context. The resulting hidden state is the intermediate numerical description at a position. “Hidden” means internal to the computation, not a secret reasoning transcript.

    2. Use attention to mix relevant context

    Attention lets one position combine information from other available positions. Three learned transformations create a query for the position doing the reading, a key for each position it might read, and a value carrying the information to combine. A query is compared with keys to obtain compatibility scores. This is a numerical matching operation, not a literal English question.

    Use a tiny query q=[1,1] and keys [1,0], [0,1], and [1,1]. A dot product multiplies corresponding coordinates and adds: the first comparison is 1×1+1×0=1. The other scores are 1 and 2. Dividing by the square root of the head width, here √2≈1.414, gives approximately [0.707,0.707,1.414].

    A softmax turns several scores into nonnegative shares that sum to one. Exponentiate each score, then divide each result by their total. Here that gives approximately [0.248,0.248,0.503]. The last position receives about half of the mixture. Unlike independent sigmoid scores, these shares compete for one total of 100%.

    The toy values are [2,0], [0,2], and [1,1]. Multiply each value by its attention share and add coordinate by coordinate. The first output coordinate is 0.248×2 + 0.248×0 + 0.503×1≈1; the second is also about 1. The actual unrounded shares produce [1,1]. Change the query in the plot and watch both the shares and the mixture change.

    An attention head is one such query/key/value computation. Multiple heads can form different mixtures. A causal mask prevents a position from reading future positions, because those future tokens would be unavailable when generating new text. Later transformations combine and reshape the mixtures. An attention weight alone is therefore not a complete explanation of why a factual claim was generated.

    3. Score the possible next tokens

    After the layers, the last available hidden state is mapped to one score for each vocabulary token. These unrestricted scores are logits. Softmax converts them into a next-token distribution. For three toy possibilities with logits [2,1,0], the shares are about [0.665,0.245,0.090].

    Greedy decoding selects the largest score. Sampling draws from the distribution instead, so a less likely token can occasionally be selected. Temperature controls how concentrated this distribution is: dividing the logits by 0.5 gives [4,2,0] and shares around [0.867,0.117,0.016]. Lower temperature makes the highest-scored token more dominant; it does not prove that token completes a true statement. The inspected Replays helpers do not explicitly set temperature.

    4. Append the token and repeat

    The chosen token becomes part of the context for the next step. This repeats until an end condition or output limit. Autoregressive is the technical name for predicting the next part using the parts already available. The model generates a recap through many such choices, not by retrieving an entire correct paragraph in one operation.

    Suppose the first selected token had probability 0.5 and the next had probability 0.4 given that first token. The probability of that two-token path is 0.5×0.4=0.2. This is what the long product equation means. The vertical bar in P(next | context) reads “given,” and the product symbol means “multiply the conditional probabilities.” It does not assume all token choices are independent.

    5. Reuse intermediate work while generating

    Prefill processes the known prompt before the first new token. Decoding produces the output tokens. Prompt positions can be processed together with the correct mask; the next generated token must wait for the previous choice.

    A key-value or KV cache saves the historical keys and values at each layer. On a later step, the model can reuse them instead of recreating every earlier position's keys and values. It still computes the new token and compares its query with available history. This differs from Redis saving a complete recap: KV caching reuses work inside generation; a recap-cache hit can skip generation.

    Longer prompts require more context processing and cache storage. Longer outputs require more sequential decoding steps. That explains why Replays selects evidence first. Removing an irrelevant play can save work; removing the decisive play can make a faithful explanation impossible. The optimization target is enough supported information, delivered efficiently.

    Choose the newsletter before writing it

    A fan following the Owls should see a different newsletter from a fan following the Foxes. The writer cannot make that choice reliably if it receives an indiscriminate pile of games. Replays first ranks the candidate items: it assigns each a relevance score, sorts them, and gives the selected evidence to the writer.

    Turn preferences into five numbers

    A feature is one measurable input to a decision. Here the five features describe whether the fan follows a team, whether they follow a player, how recent the item is, its magnitude, and whether it concerns an upcoming event. Yes/no values become 1 or 0. Magnitude is restricted to the range 0–1. These five numbers, kept in a fixed order, form a vector—an ordered list of numbers.

    Each feature has a weight, which says how much that input contributes. The score calculation multiplies matching feature/weight pairs and adds the results. This operation is a dot product. A separate starting value, the bias or intercept, is added even before any feature contribution.

    What does training actually change?

    Training uses examples to adjust the weights. For a simple engagement label, y=1 means the fan engaged and y=0 means they did not. A loss assigns a penalty to the prediction. An optimizer changes weights to reduce that penalty across examples. Inference then uses the resulting fixed weights on new items.

    One small illustration starts with two features [1,0.5], weights [0.2,−0.4], and bias zero. The weighted total is 0.2−0.2=0, giving prediction 0.5. If the observed label is 1, the prediction is too low. For logistic loss, its error signal is prediction minus label: 0.5−1=−0.5.

    Multiply that error by each feature to get the weight-change directions: [−0.5,−0.25]. A gradient-descent step subtracts a small fraction of these directions. With a step size of 0.1, the weights become [0.25,−0.375] and the bias becomes 0.05. The new total is 0.1125 and its sigmoid is about 0.5281. The score moved toward the observed label. This explains the direction of learning; it is not a reproduction of the training script's solver.

    Adapting the writer with LoRA

    A general language model already knows how to write, but it may not reliably produce Replays' particular broadcast structure or style. Fine-tuning means adjusting some model parameters using task examples. The company still needs trustworthy facts; adaptation changes how a model responds to its input, rather than creating a live sports database inside it.

    Start with an input and a desired answer

    The training pipeline collects facts, asks a teacher model for a draft, filters that draft, and stores an input–answer pair. The teacher is the model producing examples; the student is the model being adapted. This is a form of distillation: transferring behavior through examples. In this repository the targets are written answers, not the teacher's complete token probabilities.

    During training, the student predicts each target token using the prompt and the correct earlier target tokens. This is called teacher forcing. A loss gives a larger penalty when the student assigns a low probability to the desired token. If that probability is 0.2, the negative natural log is about 1.609; if it rises to 0.5, the penalty falls to about 0.693. The natural log is the inverse of raising the number e to a power; here its useful property is that it strongly penalizes very unlikely desired tokens.

    The total training objective averages these penalties over the tokens selected for loss. A loss mask specifies which tokens count. Training only on the assistant answer differs from also training on the prompt text. The inspected script does not explicitly establish assistant-only masking, so the installed trainer's configuration matters.

    Change a small addition to the model

    A model contains many learned matrices. A matrix is a table that transforms an input list of numbers into an output list. Full fine-tuning would update all selected matrix entries. LoRA, short for low-rank adaptation, keeps a base matrix fixed and learns an additional transformation represented by two smaller matrices.

    Use a tiny base matrix W=[[1,0],[0,1],[1,1]] and input h=[2,1]. Multiply each row by the input, pairing entries and adding: the first output is 1×2+0×1=2, the second is 1, and the third is 3. Thus Wh=[2,1,3].

    Let the first adapter row be A=[0.5,−0.5]. It compresses the input into one number: Ah=0.5×2−0.5×1=0.5. Let the second adapter be the column B=[0.2,−0.4,0.6]. Multiplying it by 0.5 expands the intermediate value into [0.1,−0.2,0.3].

    At adapter scale two, double that addition and add it to the base output: [2,1,3]+[0.2,−0.4,0.6]=[2.2,0.6,3.6]. Move the scale to zero to recover the unchanged base. The plot shows the matrix entries responsible for this change.

    Rank describes the number of independent directions available to the update. Here the intermediate representation contains one number, so this is a rank-one adapter. It can change several output coordinates, but their changes are linked through that one intermediate value. “Rank one” does not mean “one changed weight.”

    Now the compact equation has a concrete meaning. W is the fixed base, A compresses the input, B expands it, r is adapter rank, and α sets its scale. A prime means the adapted version.

    W′h = Wh + (α/r)B(Ah)
    adapted output = original output + scaled adapter addition

    The actual recipe uses rank 16 and alpha 32, so its scale is two. For a 4,096-by-4,096 matrix, the full matrix has 16,777,216 entries. Two rank-16 adapter matrices have 16×4096 + 4096×16 = 131,072 trainable entries, about 0.78% as many. The frozen base still occupies memory and participates in computation.

    What the optimizer and memory settings do

    Backpropagation works backward through the computation to calculate how changing each trainable entry would change loss. The optimizer uses these gradients to adjust A and B. W remains fixed. An epoch is a pass through the training examples; the recipe defaults to three epochs and learning rate 0.0002. A learning rate controls update size.

    A microbatch is the small group processed at once. With microbatch one and sixteen accumulation steps, gradients from sixteen successive examples can contribute to one optimizer update. Gradient checkpointing saves memory by retaining fewer intermediate states and recomputing some later. Optional QLoRA stores frozen base weights in a compact four-bit format while adapters train. These reduce different memory components; output length and temporary computation still cost memory.

    The distillation pipeline supplies teacher-written targets, rather than the teacher's complete probability distribution. Conceptually, supervised training minimizes negative log-likelihood on the tokens selected for loss:

    LSFT(θ) = −[1/Σi,tmit] Σi,t mit log Pθ(yit | xi, yi,<t)

Freezing weights means not updating them; their computations still participate in the forward pass and in propagating gradients to trainable adapters.

    What the batch and memory settings buy

    With microbatch one and accumulation sixteen on one device, the trainer can process sixteen examples in separate forward/backward passes before one optimizer update. Properly scaled gradients accumulate across those passes. This increases the nominal examples per update while avoiding simultaneous storage of all sixteen examples' activations. Variable sequence lengths and token-based loss reduction can affect the exact weighting.

    Gradient checkpointing addresses a different memory component: it retains fewer intermediate activations and recomputes some of them during backpropagation. Quantizing the frozen base addresses weight storage. LoRA reduces trainable parameters and their associated optimizer state. These techniques can be combined because they reduce different parts of memory consumption; none makes an arbitrarily long example free.

    Finally, a lower token loss says the model assigns more probability to the accepted targets under the training setup. If the target incorrectly says Player B scored 30, training can make that error more likely. The data filter and held-out factual evaluation therefore determine whether successful optimization advances the company's actual goal.

    One correct pick should produce one award

    State is information the product remembers after an interaction: a pick, its result, and the fan's points. Suppose a correct pick should earn ten points. If a fan refreshes, or two requests arrive together, the product should still award ten in total. This is a separate problem from generating good prose.

    An operation is atomic when other callers cannot observe or interrupt its logical steps partway through. Idempotent means repeating the same logical operation has the same effect as applying it once. A unique browser identifier helps name an operation, but does not prove a person's identity; that would require authentication. A Redis hash stores named fields, while a sorted set keeps entries ordered by a numeric score.

    Fan predictions introduce correctness that has little to do with language modeling. A resolved pick should update a profile and leaderboard once, even when requests race or a worker fails.

    The browser stores an anonymous UUID and sends it as X-Device-Id. That provides browser continuity rather than authentication. Redis hashes hold picks and profile counters, and a sorted set holds leaderboard points. Without REDIS_URL, storage operations are no-ops rather than durable success.

    A crash produces the opposite failure. If the resolved marker is written before the points update, a retry sees the marker and returns without awarding. Moving the marker after the increment would create a duplication window instead. Reordering alone cannot provide the desired guarantee.

    A proposed fix is one atomic transition using a Redis script or an optimistic transaction with conflict retries. The transition must protect every affected counter, not just the marker. Another design records an idempotent award under a logical device-and-game key and derives counters with reconciliation.

    A transaction groups changes into one logical operation. An optimistic transaction first reads the current state, then commits only if that state has not changed; a conflict requires rereading and trying again. An award ledger instead records each logical award under a unique key, so totals can be rebuilt or reconciled from those records. These approaches need different recovery rules, but both make the unit of correctness larger than a single counter increment.

    Corrected results require their own policy: reverse an earlier award, apply a versioned adjustment, or freeze an explicitly documented outcome. Authentication remains separate from atomicity; knowing an award happened once does not establish who owns a client-supplied identifier.

    A sentence can contain the right tokens and the wrong fact

    A fluent recap can sound convincing while attributing the right number to the wrong player. Evaluation asks whether each stage did its actual job, using examples whose answers can be checked. One overall “AI accuracy” number would hide where a failure entered the system.

    Check relationships, not just familiar words

    The repository's teacher-data filter searches for names and numbers in the supplied facts. Suppose the facts say Player A scored 30 and Player B scored 12. “Player B scored 30” contains familiar tokens but expresses the wrong relationship. A useful fact check must connect subject, statistic, value, time, and evidence.

    A false acceptance occurs when the filter lets an incorrect draft through. A false rejection occurs when it discards a correct draft. The current filter ignores numbers below 13 and years, permits a name when either detected token appears, and tolerates one detected violation. Its acceptance percentage therefore cannot be treated as a factuality percentage. A labeled sample of accepted and rejected drafts is needed to measure both error types.

    Measure finding enough facts as well as avoiding inventions

    Precision asks, “Of the items the system selected or claimed, how many were correct?” Recall asks, “Of the important correct items available, how many did it retain?” If ten important plays exist, a selector returns six, and five of those six are important, precision is 5/6≈83% and recall is 5/10=50%.

    The distinction matters for a recap. Saying almost nothing can avoid errors while omitting the entire turning point. Selection should measure key-event coverage; the writer should measure support for its statements; playback should check that the right narration matches the right clip. These are different questions, so a strong result on one cannot substitute for the others.

    Reward useful items near the top of a ranking

    For a newsletter, order matters. A useful item at the bottom may never be seen. Discounted cumulative gain, or DCG, gives each item a usefulness score, reduces its contribution when it appears lower down, and adds the contributions.

    Consider displayed relevance labels [3,0,2]. First convert each label to a gain using “two raised to the label, minus one”: [7,0,3]. Next divide by a position discount. Position one uses 1, position two uses log₂3≈1.585, and position three uses 2. log₂3 means “the power to which two must be raised to produce three.”

    1. Displayed order: 7/1 + 0/1.585 + 3/2 = 8.5.
    2. Best possible order of these same items, [3,2,0]: 7/1 + 3/1.585 + 0/2 ≈ 8.893.
    3. Divide the displayed score by the best possible score: 8.5/8.893≈0.956.

    That ratio is normalized DCG, or NDCG. A value of one means the order is ideal for these candidates and labels. It does not mean the newsletter satisfies 100% of fans or contains every relevant game. A missing candidate cannot be repaired by reordering the items that remain.

    Keep source facts identical when comparing writers. Otherwise a model with better input data may appear better for a reason unrelated to its writing ability. Include fallback responses and failures in product reporting, and identify unmeasured quantities explicitly.     Find the bottleneck before adding workers

    At the final buzzer, many fans may ask for the same recap. Some requests reuse a saved answer; others wait for sports data and a writer. Latency is how long a request takes from the fan's perspective. Throughput is how much work the service completes per unit time. A change can improve one without improving the other.

    An average can hide the slow experience

    Imagine 100 requests. Eighty are cache hits taking one millisecond each; twenty are misses taking ten seconds each. Convert the fast time into seconds: one millisecond is 0.001 seconds. The average is (80×0.001 + 20×10)/100 = 2.0008 seconds.

    The median is the middle of the sorted request times. Here it is 0.001 seconds because most requests are fast. The 95th percentile, or p95, is a time by which at least 95% of requests have finished. Here p95 is ten seconds: the fast requests account for only 80%, so the 95th request falls in the slow group.

    Move the hit rate and compare the average with p95. The sharp percentile change comes from this invented two-speed population; real services have a spread of times. The point is that a two-second mean does not promise a two-second wait to every fan. These values are a teaching example, not measured Replays performance.

    Written compactly, let h be the fraction of hits, Tₕ their average time, and Tₘ the average miss time. Then:

    average time = h × Tₕ + (1 − h) × Tₘ

    The symbol E[T] in the fuller equation below means expected, or average, time. The same weighted-average idea applies to per-request cost. It cannot be applied to p95 in the same way, because a percentile is a position in the sorted population, not an arithmetic average.

    Find the stage that is actually making requests wait

    A request can wait in a queue before its work begins, wait for ESPN, wait for a model, and wait for storage. A server worker is a running execution unit that handles requests. Adding workers can help if application capacity is the limit; it can also duplicate cache misses and send more calls to an already overloaded provider.

    An event loop coordinates tasks that pause while waiting for work such as network I/O. An asynchronous function can yield to other tasks, but a blocking synchronous operation inside it can still stop that loop. The inspected asynchronous endpoint calls a synchronous recap function. An appropriate thread or job mechanism would separate that blocking work; merely adding the word “async” does not change the called function.

    In a stable illustrative system, two jobs arrive per second and each spends ten seconds in the system. On average, about twenty jobs are present: 2 jobs/second × 10 seconds = 20 jobs. This relation is Little's law. It describes a stable average; if arrivals persistently exceed completion capacity, the queue can keep growing instead.

    Count failed attempts as part of time and cost

    A sequential fallback waits for one attempt to fail before starting the next. If the first model spends eight seconds timing out and the replacement succeeds in two seconds, the fan waited about ten seconds, plus other work. The successful model's two seconds alone would misdescribe the experience.

    Suppose every request incurs first-attempt cost C₁, a fraction q₁ reach attempt two, and a fraction q₂ of those reach attempt three. Average writer cost is C₁ + q₁C₂ + q₁q₂C₃. The product q₁q₂ is the fraction reaching the third attempt. q₂ is conditional on reaching attempt two, so no independence assumption is needed.

    For Replays, useful measurements separate same-game bursts from many-game bursts, hits from misses, and each provider attempt from the final response. Bounded concurrency limits how much work runs at once; shared deadlines limit total waiting; retries need spacing so they do not amplify an outage. These are proposed improvements to test against measured bottlenecks, not evidence that more compute will automatically make the product faster.

    Inference economics depend on reuse and fallback

    Let h be the full-recap cache-hit fraction, Chit the cost of serving a hit, and Cmiss the average cost of fetching and generating on a miss. A simple workload model is:

    E[C per request] = hChit + (1−h)Cmiss

    The latency identity concerns the mean, not p95. A small fraction of very slow misses can dominate the tail while barely changing a median. Increasing hit rate also helps only when the cached artifact remains valid for the source snapshot and personalization context.

    For a three-stage sequential writer chain, let q1 be the first attempt's failure probability and q2 the conditional failure probability of the second given that it is reached. Expected writer cost is C₁ + q₁C₂ + q₁q₂C₃ when each C is the appropriate conditional attempt cost. Latency must include the time spent on failed attempts, including timeout behavior.

These are sizing examples, not production measurements. For a company serving fans around game endings, hit/miss mix, burst concurrency, and cost per completed experience are necessary context for any inference-speed claim.

    The technical decisions serve one fan experience

    Replays AI's company thesis is a personal sports desk that understands what a fan follows and fits the explanation into the time they have. The data and model architecture exists to deliver that experience consistently.

    The equations now make the responsibilities explicit. Logistic proxies encode assumptions about game state. The curation model scores relevance. Autoregressive inference turns selected evidence into language. Supervised adaptation changes the writer's behavior toward accepted targets. Cache and fallback policies determine how much repeated work the service performs.

    The next useful improvement depends on which stage limits the experience. Better data and event selection help when the final stop is missing. Better supervision helps when the writer misstates an available fact. Better batching or reuse helps when the right answer takes too long or costs too much. Those are different experiments even when the UI presents one continuous broadcast.

    The durable goal is not simply more generated sports content. It is a fan who can finish an experience, understand the game they cared about, and trust the important details. That is the outcome against which the inference and data choices should be judged.
