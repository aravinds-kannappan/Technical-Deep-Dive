    Synthure and Replays: engineering systems around language models

    Two company stories, followed through their technical decisions: Synthure begins with hospital conversations about understanding, coordination, and the cost of care. Replays AI builds a personal sports desk through data selection, ranking, model inference, and reliable delivery.

    All three cheatsheets · Read Synthure separately · Read Replays separately

    The same engineering question in two domains

    A clinical note and a sports event feed seem far apart. Yet both applications transform source evidence into language for a particular reader. Their difficult failures often occur where the meaning of that evidence changes: a negated symptom becomes a diagnosis, a missing score becomes zero, or a final play disappears before the writer sees it.

    This combined edition follows Synthure first and Replays second. It retains the full mathematical explanations and code excerpts from the standalone articles so the comparison rests on actual mechanisms. The closing section draws out the shared design decisions.

    The intended reader knows basic programming and wants to understand how models fit into a working application. Equations are introduced alongside the question they answer. Reported measurements remain tied to their source datasets; proposed changes are distinguished from implemented behavior.

    Part I · Synthure

    A clinical note can support several useful reports. The difficult part is keeping its meaning intact as the text becomes entities, codes, scores, and generated explanations.

    The conversations that led to Synthure

    While volunteering at a hospital, I asked people about their pain points. Three themes came up: patients did not always understand their care; administrative work suffered from poor communication; and people struggled to find the right provider and understand whether their coverage and spending fit their needs.

    These were connected problems. An explanation that a patient could not understand could become another question for the care team. Missing information could become another administrative handoff. Choosing a provider without understanding coverage and cost could leave someone with a decision that looked reasonable medically but made little sense financially.

    That was the motivation for Synthure. The question was broader than whether AI could summarize a clinical note. Could the information from an encounter become something useful to every person who needed to act on it, without making each person reconstruct the same story?

    The financial part of that question also goes beyond estimating a bill. A person wants care that fits their profile, a provider appropriate for their needs, and confidence that they are not paying more than necessary. A useful product has to connect clinical needs, coverage, provider suitability, and cost. A model that simply chooses the cheapest number would not solve that problem.

    The implementation examined here develops a foundation for that ambition: turn a messy note into a reviewable encounter record, connect that record to coding and reference data, and derive different views from the same state. Full personalized coverage and provider matching require additional data and validation; they are part of the larger product direction, rather than a capability established by the current code.

    This article starts with those hospital conversations and the product decisions they imply. The technical sections then follow the same questions: how to preserve what happened, how to identify what is missing, how to connect it to useful reference information, and how to keep every view consistent when someone makes a correction.

    Three needs, one underlying information problem

    Start with the patient. A note may be perfectly readable to a clinician and still fail as an explanation. The patient needs to understand what was found, which decisions remain open, what the next step is, and what information could change that step. Expanding abbreviations helps, but the more important task is preserving the distinction between a confirmed condition and a possibility.

    For example, “knee surgery is being considered” supports an explanation of a possible next step. It does not support instructions that assume surgery happened. This is why Synthure needs structured evidence before it needs polished language. The patient's experience depends on whether the software preserves status, uncertainty, and timing.

    Now consider the administrative team. A question such as “Is authorization on file?” has an owner, a state, and consequences for other people. If it is answered in one portal while another still shows it as unresolved, generating a better summary creates little value. The system needs a shared state transition that other views can observe.

    The provider-and-coverage problem is more demanding still. A diagnosis can help identify an appropriate kind of care, but it cannot determine plan eligibility, network participation, provider availability, a negotiated price, or whether a different option is a better fit. Those are separate inputs. Combining them responsibly requires explicit data joins and update policies, not asking a language model to infer missing insurance facts.

    What people neededWhat the product must representTechnical consequence

    This mapping determines the order of the engineering. First establish a reliable account of the encounter. Then connect the account to rules and reference data. Only then use it to support decisions and explanations. Beginning with a provider recommendation before those inputs exist would produce confidence faster than it produces knowledge.

    Why this is still hard when healthcare already has so much software

    There is no shortage of products for notes, coding, revenue-cycle work, or patient communication. Abridge describes encounter-grounded documentation and revenue-cycle capabilities, and Ambience exposes documentation and coding capabilities through APIs. These are examples of related offerings, not an exhaustive market comparison. Synthure's case cannot rest on a claim that nobody has attempted these problems.

    The product thesis is about continuity: preserve the meaning of the encounter as it moves between people, and let a verified change affect all the views that depend on it. That is a goal to demonstrate in a workflow, rather than an assertion of exclusive functionality.

    One obstacle is that each workflow asks a different question of the same note. A clinical phrase may be enough to explain a condition but insufficient to assign a specific code. A valid code may still leave missing documentation. Complete documentation does not establish coverage. Coverage does not establish that a particular provider is available or the best fit. These implications stop at different boundaries.

    Another obstacle is that the evidence changes at different rates. Clinical facts can be corrected during review. Plan benefits can change between coverage periods. Provider participation and availability need current sources. An explanation generated last week may be grammatically perfect while depending on an obsolete assumption.

    Access also differs by role. A shared internal representation does not imply that every reader should receive every field. The clinician's detailed view and an employer-facing aggregate view need different disclosure rules. Redacting an input is one useful operation; controlling what each downstream view can reveal is another.

    Finally, the feedback needed to learn each task is different. Mention-to-code examples can train a linker. They do not train a provider matcher or establish savings. A readiness label does not reveal whether a payer later accepted a claim. A company has to earn each of these claims with the appropriate data and outcome measurement.

    This explains the initial scope of Synthure: clinical-note normalization and a claim-readiness copilot. It is a concrete place to make the shared-information idea useful and testable. The broader matching ambition can build on that foundation when eligibility, provider, price, and outcome data are available.

    The first product decision: make the encounter the unit of work

    Four separate AI summaries would reproduce the fragmentation that motivated the company. Each could reinterpret the same phrase, choose different details, and become stale after a correction. Synthure instead builds a shared record and uses it to support patient, physician, hospital, and employer views.

    The record begins with the note, but grows beyond text. It includes supported entities, candidate codes, documentation gaps, procedures, and reference values. The product layer adds accepted or rejected items, plan inputs, authorization state, and actions taken during review. A view is a projection of that state for a particular reader.

    The browser runs local redaction and biomedical extraction. The server adds lookups, compact-model predictions, and hosted narration. These are different kinds of computation. Extraction proposes what the note says; lookup connects it to a catalog; prediction estimates a labeled outcome; narration explains the resulting record.

    note → cleaned text → supported mentions → candidate codes and gaps
    → reviewable encounter → derived workflow state → audience-specific explanation

    That division is a response to the original pain points. Local processing limits raw-note transmission in the normal browser workflow. Constrained catalog selection narrows coding errors. A shared encounter makes a correction reusable. Audience-specific writers help with comprehension after the evidence has been assembled.

    The architecture creates a useful debugging question: where did meaning change? If every view incorrectly marks a proposed procedure as completed, inspect the common record. If only the patient explanation changes its status, inspect the writer. If a reviewed correction fails to reach another portal, inspect state propagation. The product problem gives each technical boundary a purpose.

    From an attractive prediction to a defensible task

    The repository records a revealing change in direction. An earlier version used note keywords to produce a denial-risk score. The README explains that this score was removed. The implemented direction separates documentation readiness and explicit checks from an unsupported probability of denial.

    Why does that matter? Predicting a denial would require a target such as the eventual outcome of a submitted claim, linked to the information available when the prediction was made. A model trained on generated documentation labels learns a different task. Calling its output a denial probability would not change the supervision it received.

    Readiness is narrower and more actionable. Is a required detail missing? Is the code valid? Is an authorization requirement unresolved in the record? These questions can lead to a specific review task. A clinician or coder can inspect the reason and supply the missing information.

    The current system contains both a learned documentation-readiness predictor and deterministic readiness checks. They should remain distinguishable. The predictor summarizes patterns in its training label; the checks expose individually inspectable conditions. Neither certifies coverage or establishes the outcome of an external claim.

    This evolution connects the company motivation to model design. The ambition is to reduce confusion and unnecessary work. A score contributes only if its meaning is clear enough for someone to use and challenge. Otherwise it creates another opaque artifact that staff must interpret.

    From token predictions to clinical assertions

    Named-entity recognition identifies spans such as conditions, medications, or identifiers. OpenMed produces token-level predictions; the application needs character ranges that can be highlighted, redacted, and checked. The browser uses ONNX models with int8 quantization, which reduces weight storage relative to higher-precision weights. Actual runtime benefits still depend on the browser and execution backend.

    Text:     chronic      kidney       disease
Labels:   B-DISEASE    I-DISEASE     I-DISEASE
Meaning:  beginning   continuation  continuation

Merged mention: "chronic kidney disease"
Next question: Is it current, negated, uncertain, or historical?

    BIO tags mark the beginning and continuation of an entity. The aggregation code joins compatible pieces, removes WordPiece’s ## marker, and searches forward for the reconstructed phrase. It assigns the minimum token score to a merged span. This conservative aggregation prevents a weak token from being hidden by an average, although it is not a calibrated probability of correct clinical interpretation.

    Character ranges use the half-open convention [start, end). If a name at [5,10) is replaced by twelve characters, a later span moves seven positions. Applying edits from right to left preserves the offsets of earlier spans that are still waiting to be replaced:

    export async function deidentify(text: string, minScore = 0.5): Promise<DeidResult> {

  // Replace right to left so earlier spans keep their offsets.
  let clean = text
  const types = new Set<string>()

    types.add(tag)

    The default redaction threshold is 0.5; disease and pharmaceutical extraction use 0.6. The latter passes run in parallel, and duplicate (start,end,type) spans retain the higher confidence. Those thresholds trade missed mentions against false detections. Their numerical values alone do not establish the best operating point.

    Two failure types deserve separate tests. “Pain improved; pain returned” tests whether the extractor locates the intended occurrence. “No fever but persistent cough” tests whether negation stops at the right boundary. Tokenizer offsets, where available, help with the first. Assertion modeling helps with the second. A forward text search and nearby negation rules address only parts of these problems.

    Abbreviation expansion and RxNorm lookup add useful vocabulary support. They cannot resolve every ambiguous abbreviation or determine whether a medication is current. A stronger record would attach assertion status—current, historical, negated, uncertain, proposed—to each supported mention. This is a proposed extension, and it becomes especially valuable when mentions enter the coding stage.

    Why some decisions fit small models

    Identifying a note category or checking for missing documentation is a narrower task than writing a patient explanation. Synthure serves these decisions using exported weights in TypeScript. This avoids a hosted generation call and makes the computation inspectable.

    Note type: TF-IDF and a linear classifier

    The classifier counts unigrams and bigrams from an exported vocabulary. For an observed term with count c, its unnormalized weight is (1 + ln c) × IDF. The vector is L2-normalized before applying class coefficients and intercepts:

    zj = bj + wjTx
    pj = exp(zj − max z) / Σk exp(zk − max z)

    Here x is the normalized feature vector, and z contains class logits. Subtracting the largest logit prevents numerical overflow without changing the softmax ratios.

    The training and serving implementations must agree on vocabulary indices, IDF values, class order, and feature construction. Equal vector lengths are insufficient. Swapping two feature positions still produces valid arithmetic with the wrong meaning. If no vocabulary term matches, intercepts alone determine the output, which is another reason to track evidence coverage alongside confidence.

    Missing fields: several labels can be true

    Missing-field detection uses an ordered structural vector: counts, lexical flags, normalized note length, and one-hot note type. Each field has a sigmoid classifier, σ(z)=1/(1+exp(−z)). Separate sigmoids allow dosage and laterality to be missing simultaneously; a softmax would unnecessarily force the labels to compete.

    The returned present flag means the missing-field prediction exceeded 0.5. It does not mean the medical detail was supplied. There is also a representational limit: “Left arm examined; knee pain” contains a side, but not the knee’s side. Numerical parity with training cannot repair a feature representation that loses this association.

    Inference is the execution of a learned decision rule

    The distinction between training and inference is concrete here. Training estimates the vocabulary, IDF values, and coefficients from examples. Inference holds those artifacts fixed, constructs one feature vector, and computes logits. For C classes and m nonzero features, the sparse scoring loop costs roughly O(Cm) arithmetic after tokenization, rather than a hosted autoregressive generation.

    That makes these models suitable for frequent, narrow decisions in the workflow. It also makes model versioning inseparable from preprocessing versioning. Deploying new coefficients with an old vocabulary changes the meaning of every multiplication. A useful export test compares intermediate feature vectors and logits between training and serving, before softmax or rounding can hide discrepancies.

    Retrieving a code is easier than establishing that it applies

    A catalog makes coding a constrained selection problem. Instead of allowing the writer to invent an identifier, Synthure retrieves candidate terms and ranks their codes. This guarantees a more useful kind of output space, but a valid code can still be wrong for the note.

    The lexical path uses an inverted index: tokens point to catalog terms containing them. icdCandidates expands abbreviations, tries all unique query tokens, relaxes to one fewer, and finally backs off to strong tokens. Ordering accounts for overlap, agreement with the head word, and extra words in the candidate.

    The route requests up to eight candidates. A logistic reranker scores overlap, term length, billability, and retrieval rank; the best candidate must clear 0.15. Codes are then checked against the reference and deduplicated.

    This path calls the local rerankScore, despite a source comment describing language-model selection. The distinction matters: these four features do not give the scorer a full reading of the encounter.

    Retrieval places a ceiling on reranking. Suppose the correct code enters the shortlist for 90 of 100 mentions, and the reranker selects it for 72. Overall accuracy is 72%; conditional accuracy among successful retrievals is 80%. The remaining ten retrieval misses require a better candidate set, regardless of reranker capacity.

    For “right-knee osteoarthritis,” laterality is supported. For “knee osteoarthritis,” a side-specific result needs evidence the phrase lacks. Billability and a high overlap score do not supply it. Useful improvements would measure recall@8 first, then test qualifier-aware acceptance and abstention on ambiguous near-ties.

    Learning similarity when words do not overlap

    Lexical retrieval is easy to inspect, but synonymous phrases may share few tokens. The optional neural coder learns vectors for mentions and code descriptions so that matching meanings can receive high similarity even when their surface wording differs.

    A bi-encoder processes the mention and description separately. It mean-pools token states with the attention mask and normalizes the resulting vectors. Catalog descriptions can be embedded ahead of time; a request only needs a query embedding and comparisons with the saved catalog vectors. For unit vectors, their dot product equals cosine similarity.

    A cross-encoder instead processes each mention–description pair together. This permits richer word interactions, but incurs a model pass per pair. A common division of labor is therefore cheap broad retrieval followed by expensive joint scoring on a shortlist.

    The training objective

    Let ui be mention i’s vector and vj description j’s vector. For a batch of paired examples, the similarity matrix is Sij=uiTvj/τ, where temperature τ controls how sharply scores compete. The correct pairs occupy the diagonal.

    Lmention→code = −(1/B) Σi log [exp(Sii) / Σj allowed exp(Sij)]

    Usually, off-diagonal examples serve as negatives. But two mentions in a batch may map to the same code. Penalizing those pairs would teach the model to separate legitimate synonyms. This implementation masks same-code collisions and averages cross-entropy in both retrieval directions:

    ml/icd_coder/model.py · lines 45–58 · source revision a9f829bb · selected excerpt

    def info_nce(anchor: torch.Tensor, positive: torch.Tensor, codes: torch.Tensor, temperature: float) -> torch.Tensor:
    """Symmetric in-batch InfoNCE.

    anchor[i] and positive[i] are the phrase and its code's description. Any j!=i
    with the same code is masked out so a true synonym is never punished as a
    negative (in-batch collisions are common in a 98k-code space).
    """
    logits = anchor @ positive.t() / temperature           # (B, B)
    same = codes.unsqueeze(0) == codes.unsqueeze(1)         # (B, B) same code
    eye = torch.eye(logits.size(0), dtype=torch.bool, device=logits.device)
    mask = same & ~eye
    logits = logits.masked_fill(mask, float("-inf"))
    target = torch.arange(logits.size(0), device=logits.device)
    return 0.5 * (F.cross_entropy(logits, target) + F.cross_entropy(logits.t(), target))

    Batch construction is consequently part of the learning problem. Hard negatives can improve discrimination between related codes; false negatives can damage the very equivalence the encoder should learn. Increasing the encoder’s size does not resolve incorrect labels.

    The neural route still inherits the retrieval ceiling. More candidates increase the chance that the correct code reaches a joint scorer and increase scoring cost. The repository contains the training and optional serving path; that does not establish that a trained endpoint served every request. It also should not be confused with an implemented retrieval system over payer-policy documents. Those would require their own corpus, versioning, retrieval evaluation, and evidence links.

    Turning a model score into a review policy

    Our example still has incomplete medication details and an unknown authorization status. A readiness predictor can help prioritize review, but cannot turn those unknowns into facts. Its job is to estimate a labeled documentation outcome from the features available.

    Synthure evaluates exported gradient-boosted trees. Starting from a bias, it traverses each tree to a leaf and adds that leaf’s value multiplied by the learning rate. A sigmoid converts the accumulated logit into the raw score:

    praw = σ(b + η Σm leafm(x))

    For a toy ensemble with bias −0.4, learning rate 0.1, and selected leaves 1.2 and −0.3, the logit is −0.31 and the score is about 0.423. This is a fitted prediction, not the fraction of documentation rules that passed.

    function gbmProb(x: number[]): number {
  const gbm = (readinessModel as { gbm: { trees: Tree[]; learning_rate: number; init: number } }).gbm
  let s = gbm.init
  for (const t of gbm.trees) {
    let node = 0
    while (t.children_left[node] !== -1) {
      node = x[t.feature[node]] <= t.threshold[node] ? t.children_left[node] : t.children_right[node]
    }
    s += gbm.learning_rate * t.value[node]
  }
  return sigmoid(s)
}
function calibrate(p: number): number {
  const { x, y } = (readinessModel as { calibration: { x: number[]; y: number[] } }).calibration
  for (let i = 0; i < x.length - 1; i++) {
    if (p <= x[i + 1]) {
      const t = (p - x[i]) / (x[i + 1] - x[i] + 1e-9)
      return y[i] + t * (y[i + 1] - y[i])
    }
  }
  return y[y.length - 1]
}
export function predictReadiness(note: string, noteType: string, nDx: number, nProc: number): {
  raw: number; calibrated: number; band: 'ready' | 'needs_work' | 'not_ready'
} {
  const raw = gbmProb(structVector(note, noteType, nDx, nProc))
  const calibrated = calibrate(raw)
  const band = calibrated >= 0.66 ? 'ready' : calibrated >= 0.4 ? 'needs_work' : 'not_ready'
  return { raw: Number(raw.toFixed(3)), calibrated: Number(calibrated.toFixed(3)), band }
}

    The second function maps the raw score through calibration knots. Between (0.4,0.3) and (0.6,0.5), for example, interpolation sends 0.5 to 0.4. Calibration aims for agreement between predicted scores and observed frequencies on comparable cases. It requires separate fitting and evaluation data, and can deteriorate when the input distribution changes.

    The implementation returns the last mapped value above the last knot, but can extrapolate below the first. Boundary tests therefore matter as much as interior examples. A model can rank cases well while producing misleading probability values; AUROC and calibration measure different properties.

    The application turns calibrated scores into three bands: below 0.40 is “not ready,” 0.40 to below 0.66 is “needs work,” and 0.66 or above is “ready.” Those are policy thresholds on top of the predictor. Changing a threshold changes both accepted quality and review workload.

    In the illustrative comparison, accepting 80 cases with four errors gives a 5% accepted-case error rate. Accepting 50 with one error gives 2%, while sending 30 additional cases to review. Reporting the second percentage alone hides the cost. Coverage, error among accepted outputs, and review effort belong together.

    A score of 0.72 still cannot answer our authorization question. Readiness bands, missing-evidence rules, and the final report decision remain separate parts of the workflow.

    A correction should change every view that depends on it

    Suppose a reviewer accepts a procedure and then records that its authorization issue has been resolved. The patient needs an updated explanation, the administrative view needs an updated task state, and the financial illustration needs to be recomputed. Running four new prompts is not the mechanism that keeps those facts consistent.

    In encounter.ts, actions transform the shared state and a pure derive() function computes the values the portals read. With state s, action a, reducer R, and derived view function D:

    st+1 = R(st, at)
    viewst+1 = D(st+1)

    The action may toggle an accepted diagnosis, add or remove a procedure, update plan inputs, or record authorization. Stable fact IDs connect events to the affected diagnosis, service, or check. The reducer also retains an action log; replaying the base extraction plus those actions reconstructs derived clinical and financial state. Cosmetic event timestamps and IDs are regenerated.

    This is the technical answer to part of the communication problem: one accepted change has one state transition, and dependent values are derived from its result. It is more reliable than storing four independently edited copies of the same fact.

    Make financial arithmetic visible

    Let A be the sum of priced service estimates, D the remaining deductible, c the coinsurance fraction, and O the remaining out-of-pocket maximum. The code's simplified benefit calculation is:

    d = min(D, A)
    estimated patient amount = round(min(O, d + c(A−d)))

    For an illustrative A=$1,000, D=$300, c=0.20, and O=$800, the amount is 300 + 0.20×700 = $440. If the remaining maximum is $400, the estimate becomes $400. These examples explain the implemented arithmetic; they are not a patient quote.

    The code builds service estimates from reference prices and a selected payer multiplier. Unpriced services are excluded and surfaced as assumptions. Rounded line-item allocations can also differ slightly from the rounded total. These choices are important because a displayed total can otherwise look more complete and precise than its inputs.

    The internal “at risk” amount sums priced services with an unresolved authorization condition or no accepted supporting diagnosis. The variable called expectedReimb subtracts that amount from the total. This is a rule-based allocation, not a statistical expectation of money a payer will pay. If one of two $500 services is flagged, the displayed split is $500 and $500; clearing the flag moves that amount without fitting a new risk model.

    Similarly, an approvePriorAuth or submitClaim action updates the application record. Its name does not prove that an insurer approved anything or that a claim was transmitted. Connecting these actions to authoritative external events is necessary before the UI can make those operational claims.

    For the company, this separation creates a path forward. Keep deterministic amounts and assumptions inspectable, use models where interpretation is needed, and connect consequential state changes to verified evidence. The workflow becomes understandable to both the patient and the person reviewing it.

    What personalized coverage and provider matching would require

    The third hospital pain point asks a larger question than the current estimator answers: does a person's care arrangement fit their health needs and what they pay, and can they find an appropriate provider without unnecessary spending?

    A practical matcher would first construct an eligible candidate set. It would need verified plan participation, the relevant specialty or service, location or accessibility constraints, and actual availability. Clinical compatibility and coverage conditions belong in this filtering step. A cheap option that cannot provide the needed care is not a meaningful bargain.

    Only then would ranking make sense. A conceptual objective could combine patient cost, travel or waiting time, suitability, and confidence in the underlying information. Its weights express preferences and policy choices; they are not truths a generic model can discover without supervision.

    eligible(u) = {v : verified coverage and service constraints hold}
    v* = arg minv∈eligible(u) [estimated cost(u,v) + preference-weighted burdens(u,v)]

    Here u denotes the patient profile and v a provider option. This is a proposed problem formulation, not a deployed Synthure model. Missing coverage should remain unknown rather than be scored as a favorable zero. Costs need comparable assumptions, and provider suitability cannot be reduced to price alone.

    Assessing whether a plan fits what a person is paying introduces a longer horizon: premiums, expected use, cost sharing, and access constraints. A single encounter cannot establish that someone is overpaying across a year. A defensible recommendation would state the scenario, alternatives, uncertainty, and inputs that change the result.

    The existing note pipeline contributes one part: a reviewable description of needs and proposed services. The shared encounter contributes another: explicit preferences and assumptions that can be changed and recomputed. The provider directory, eligibility, negotiated-price, and outcome evidence needed to complete the matching task are additional integrations and evaluation work.

    This keeps the founding ambition intact without confusing an early foundation with a finished solution. The success criterion would eventually be better-fit care and understandable costs, measured against real alternatives, rather than the number of recommendations the model can generate.

    Part II · Replays AI

    A sports recap is a small data pipeline with an editorial problem inside it: decide what matters, preserve the facts, and deliver the result before the fan loses interest.

    The product starts with the fan’s time

    A fan can care deeply about a team without having time to watch every game, inspect every box score, and keep up with every player. The useful product question is how much of that work a personal sports desk can take over while still giving the fan a faithful sense of what happened.

    Replays AI's documented product direction is a personalized desk built from real NBA and NFL data: a feed, narrated reels, a two-host broadcast, picks, and a weekly newsletter. Team and player preferences give the system an initial idea of what matters without requiring an account.

    These surfaces solve different parts of the same problem. The feed helps a fan notice relevant games. A recap explains one result. A short broadcast fits a limited listening window. A newsletter connects a week's events. The company needs a consistent account of sport that can adapt to the fan's interests and available time.

    That is more demanding than producing a fluent paragraph. A model can write a convincing story about the wrong game, select the spectacular play that did not affect the result, or make correct narration impossible to fit into the chosen clip. Product quality emerges from the whole path.

    We will follow an invented 104–101 Owls–Foxes game from provider records to ranked evidence, statistical analysis, token-by-token generation, and playback. The equations below distinguish implemented scoring rules, general model mechanics, and proposed improvements. That distinction matters because “the AI” is several different computations.

    Why a personal sports desk is more than automated highlights

    Automated sports content is an existing category. For example, WSC Sports describes automated video and personalized highlight experiences. Replays' opportunity is therefore a product hypothesis about the fan experience, not a claim to have invented automated highlights.

    The organizing idea is that preferences should carry across formats. A fan who follows one team and two players should not have to reconstruct those interests in a stats page, a broadcast, and a weekly digest. The same preference profile should help decide which evidence enters each experience, while the format determines how that evidence is presented.

    The constraints explain the architecture. Sports data changes quickly, so the application needs normalization and freshness rules. A short recap has limited room, so it needs evidence selection. Fans differ, so it needs a ranker. Narration is sequential, so it needs generation and timing budgets. Popular finished games attract repeated requests, so it needs reuse and concurrency control.

    It is useful to express the goal as maximizing useful, supported information within the fan's available time. This is a design objective, not an objective currently optimized by a single learned model:

    choose content S to increase relevance and supported coverage
    subject to time budget, evidence availability, and delivery constraints

    Adding another model helps only if it improves one of those terms. More colorful commentary can lower quality if it adds unsupported tactical claims. More retrieved events can slow inference and bury the important moment. Longer broadcasts can explain more while failing the user's two-minute constraint.

    The company-level measurements would therefore include whether fans finish the selected experience, find it useful, return for their teams, and encounter factual errors. Latency and cost per useful delivered experience belong beside those outcomes. The repository does not establish measured product-market fit or those business results; it gives us the implementation with which to test the hypothesis.

    Four meanings of inference in one product

    A named agent can be a rule, a small classifier, or a language-model call. Treating them all as equivalent hides where the behavior comes from and how to evaluate it.

    ComputationInput → outputWhat determines its behavior?
    Play-analysis proxyDistance or score/time → a proxy scoreHand-set mathematical constants
    Curation rankerFive relevance features → item scoreHeuristic weights, or an exported learned weight file
    General writerFact sheet and instructions → generated tokensA hosted pretrained model and its decoding configuration

    Inference means applying the fixed computation to new inputs. Training means estimating its adjustable parameters from examples. A sigmoid can appear in either a hand-designed proxy or a trained logistic model; the presence of the same function does not give them the same provenance.

    The rest of the article follows the boundaries between these computations. Provider data establishes the facts. Selection decides what survives. Ranking decides what comes first for the fan. Generation decides how to say it. Each stage can succeed numerically while the product fails semantically, so each needs a target that matches its responsibility.

    A shared game record supports several products

    Replays uses a React frontend and a FastAPI backend. The backend reads ESPN data on demand and converts it into consistent fields. Redis, when configured, supports shared storage for artifacts and fan state. The live request path should be distinguished from historical ingestion or PostgreSQL code elsewhere in the repository.

    Fan chooses teams and players
  → browser requests a feed or game
  → backend obtains ESPN data
  → adapter converts it into consistent fields
  → rules select and analyze important events
  → writer or template produces a story
  → interface displays facts, media and source labels

    The products diverge after this common beginning. A recap needs a coherent account of one game. A newsletter first chooses which games belong in a fan’s feed. A reel needs available video and narration that fits its scenes. A broadcast needs structured turns that a player can schedule.

    Keeping these responsibilities separate makes failures easier to diagnose. A wrong score in several products suggests an adapter or source issue. A correct score attached to an irrelevant newsletter suggests selection or ranking. Correct narration over the wrong clip suggests media alignment.

    Sharing facts also creates dependencies. A corrected game record can invalidate several generated artifacts. A preference change affects personalized selection without changing the game itself. A missing clip breaks one presentation while leaving a text recap usable. Each artifact therefore needs an identity and freshness policy appropriate to what it depends on.

    Fallbacks make the design practical. Known scores can still become deterministic prose when generation fails. Newsletter and broadcast routes can attempt a configured trained writer, a general model, and then a template. The successful route needs to remain visible: a training script in the repository does not establish that a particular response used its saved adapter.

    The field-goal equation, step by step

    pkick(d) = 1 / [1 + exp((d−53.5)/6.5)]
    log[p/(1−p)] = (53.5−d)/6.5

    Here d is distance in yards. The second form is the log-odds: moving 6.5 yards farther multiplies the modeled success odds by e−1≈0.368. At 53.5 yards the log-odds are zero and the score is one-half. At 45 yards the exponent is about −1.308, producing 0.787.

    What happens inside the writer at inference time

    Once the selected fact sheet is serialized with the instructions, the writer receives a token sequence. The sports record is not used as an external truth constraint at every decoding step. It becomes context that influences the model's next-token distribution.

    Let x be that context and y a generated sequence. An autoregressive writer factorizes its probability as:

    Pθ(y | x) = ∏t=1…L Pθ(yt | x, y<t)

    At each step, a final hidden vector is projected into vocabulary logits. For token v, temperature-scaled softmax gives:

    p(v) = exp(zv/τ) / Σu∈Vexp(zu/τ)

    For illustrative logits [2,1,0] and τ=1, probabilities are approximately [0.665,0.245,0.090]. At τ=0.5, they become [0.867,0.117,0.016]. Lower temperature concentrates probability on higher-logit tokens. It cannot guarantee that the highest-logit claim is true.

    The inspected API helpers supply the model, system and user messages, and maximum output tokens. They do not explicitly set temperature. These equations explain general decoder mechanics; they are not a claim about a temperature selected by Replays or the internals of a particular hosted provider.

    How the facts influence the next token

    For a typical decoder attention head, hidden states produce query, key, and value matrices. The attention operation is:

    Q = HWQ,   K = HWK,   V = HWV
    Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKT/√dk + M)V

    The causal mask M excludes future positions. The square-root scaling moderates the magnitude of dot products as head width grows. Multiple heads, feed-forward blocks, residual connections, and normalization transform this information before the vocabulary projection.

    This gives a mechanism for a generated sentence to depend on team names, scores, and earlier generated words. It does not force “30 points” to remain attached to the correct player. Grounding requires evaluating the resulting relations against the fact sheet; attention alone is not a database join.

    Prefill and decoding have different costs

    During prefill, the model processes the prompt and builds representations of its tokens. During decoding, it generates tokens sequentially, reusing cached attention keys and values in a typical implementation. A useful latency decomposition is:

    Twriter ≈ Tqueue + Tprefill(P) + Σt=1…LTdecode(P+t)

    P is prompt length and L is generated length. Ordinary dense attention has a quadratic prompt-length interaction term during prefill; optimized kernels can change memory use without removing every arithmetic cost. Longer output also extends the sequential decoding phase.

    A typical KV-cache sizing expression is 2 × layers × batch × sequence length × KV heads × head width × bytes per element. The factor two counts keys and values. Actual serving depends on model architecture and backend; the application calls hosted endpoints and does not expose their full memory configuration.

    This connects directly to event selection. Removing irrelevant plays can shorten prefill and improve focus, but removing the decisive play damages the answer. The useful optimization is supported information per prompt token, with event coverage measured alongside latency. A short context is only better if it retains what the fan needs to understand.

    What changes when the ranker is trained?

    The training script fits logistic regression to feature vectors and binary engagement labels, then exports the coefficients. A standard conceptual form of the penalized objective is:

    L(w,b) = −Σi[yilog pi + (1−yi)log(1−pi)] + λ‖w‖²/2
    pi = σ(b+wTxi)

    The data gradient contributed by one example is (p−y)x.     The script uses real rows alone once at least 500 are supplied. Below that threshold it combines them with 4,000 synthetic examples. A fitted cold-start model learns assumptions from that oracle; the fact that optimization ran does not establish improvement for real fans.

    The script prints training accuracy rather than implementing a separate held-out ranking experiment. A stronger test would preserve exposure, timestamp, fan, and candidate-set identity; split by time and relevant groups; and compare ranking quality against the deployed baseline. An unexposed item is not a negative interaction, and a click reflects both relevance and the chance to see the item.

It does not make a single overlong example fit. Checkpointing stores fewer intermediate activations and recomputes them during backpropagation.

    The optional QLoRA path stores frozen base weights in four-bit NF4 with double quantization and bf16 compute. Adapters still train. Activations, optimizer state, and workspaces do not all become four-bit, so memory estimates must include them separately.

    The split can matter more than the rank

    The script performs a random 95/5 record split with seed 7. Multiple drafts from the same game can therefore cross the boundary. A model familiar with the game facts may look better on validation without generalizing to unseen games. Grouping by game before generation and keeping a final time-disjoint test would provide a stronger comparison.

    The excerpt does not explicitly set an evaluation cadence, best-checkpoint policy, or assistant-only loss mask. Those depend on the installed training library and configuration and should be checked before making claims about the learning objective. Long fact sheets also need truncation inspection so the sequence cap does not remove the desired answer.

    Saving an adapter establishes an artifact. Serving additionally requires the matching base revision, tokenizer, chat format, and endpoint configuration. The meaningful comparison is template, base writer, and adapted writer on identical unseen facts, measuring factuality, coverage, structure, latency, and cost.

    What the student is actually minimizing

    The distillation pipeline supplies teacher-written targets, rather than the teacher's complete probability distribution. Conceptually, supervised training minimizes negative log-likelihood on the tokens selected for loss:

    LSFT(θ) = −[1/Σi,tmit] Σi,t mit log Pθ(yit | xi, yi,<t)

    The mask m identifies which tokens contribute. An assistant-only mask trains on target responses; a full serialized-sequence loss also includes prompt tokens. The script does not explicitly establish assistant-only masking, so the installed trainer configuration must be checked before assigning it that objective.

    For one desired token assigned probability 0.2, the loss contribution is −ln(0.2)≈1.609. Raising it to 0.5 reduces the contribution to about 0.693. The optimizer rewards matching the teacher target. It does not independently know that a teacher sentence swapped two players' statistics.

    During teacher forcing, the context includes the correct previous target tokens. At generation time, the model conditions on its own previous outputs. A mistaken early name can affect later sentences. This is one reason held-out token loss and factual performance on generated broadcasts should both be measured.

    The LoRA update during a forward pass

    For input vector h, the adapted projection is Wh + (α/r)B(Ah). The base projection remains; the adapter first compresses into r coordinates and then expands to the output width. At the repository's r=16 and α=32, the multiplier is two.

    The low-rank update constrains how this matrix can change, but updates across many layers can still alter rich behavior. Increasing rank adds trainable capacity and cost; it does not repair a weak target filter. For a compatible full-precision base, the update can be merged into a weight matrix for serving. Quantized and multi-adapter deployments have backend-specific choices, so the repository's endpoint configuration alone does not establish a particular merge strategy.

    The important deployment contract is the combination of base revision, adapter, tokenizer, prompt format, and decoding policy. A new adapter attached to a different base or chat template is a different system. The product's source label should identify the route, while a reproducibility record should identify the exact artifacts that route used.

    One correct pick should produce one award

    Fan predictions introduce correctness that has little to do with language modeling. A resolved pick should update a profile and leaderboard once, even when requests race or a worker fails.

    The browser stores an anonymous UUID and sends it as X-Device-Id. That provides browser continuity rather than authentication. Redis hashes hold picks and profile counters, and a sorted set holds leaderboard points. Without REDIS_URL, storage operations are no-ops rather than durable success.

    The resolution function reads the pick, checks whether it is unresolved, writes a resolved marker, and then increments counters. Two requests can both read “unresolved” before either writes. Each increment is atomic, but the logical read–check–award operation is not.

    A crash produces the opposite failure. If the resolved marker is written before the points update, a retry sees the marker and returns without awarding. Moving the marker after the increment would create a duplication window instead. Reordering alone cannot provide the desired guarantee.

    A proposed fix is one atomic transition using a Redis script or an optimistic transaction with conflict retries. The transition must protect every affected counter, not just the marker. Another design records an idempotent award under a logical device-and-game key and derives counters with reconciliation.

    Corrected results require their own policy: reverse an earlier award, apply a versioned adjustment, or freeze an explicitly documented outcome. Authentication remains separate from atomicity; knowing an award happened once does not establish who owns a client-supplied identifier.

    Useful tests race two resolutions and inject failures at each write boundary. Responses should distinguish “already resolved,” “no award due,” and “store unavailable.” Returning zero for all three hides exactly the information recovery needs.

    A sentence can contain the right tokens and the wrong fact

    The teacher-data filter checks whether generated names and numbers occur in serialized facts. This is a useful inexpensive screen for some inventions. Its acceptance rule shows why it cannot be treated as a factuality guarantee:

    ok_tokens = _facts_tokens(blob)

            continue

            continue

            reasons.append(f"invented number {num}")

    for first, last in _NAME.findall(target):
        # A person reference is grounded if the surname appears in the facts.
        if last.lower() not in ok_tokens and first.lower() not in ok_tokens:
            reasons.append(f"invented name {first} {last}")

    # Allow a tiny amount of noise (e.g. an idiom that reads like a name).
    return (len(reasons) <= 1, reasons)

    Numbers below 13 and values in the year range are ignored. A detected two-word name passes if either token is present. Up to one detected violation is accepted.

    Suppose Player A scored 30 points and Player B scored 12. “Player B scored 30” uses an available player and an available number while stating the wrong relationship. A fabricated twelve-point statistic can bypass the numeric threshold entirely. Token membership does not establish subject–statistic agreement.

    The filter therefore needs a labeled audit of false accepts and false rejects. Its acceptance rate alone measures neither. Errors that pass are especially consequential when their text becomes training supervision.

    Evaluate each transformation against its own target

    Selection needs key-event recall and precision. Writing needs supported-claim rate and important-fact coverage. Broadcasts need schema validity, factuality, clip alignment, and measured playback timing. State needs concurrent-resolution and crash-recovery checks.

    Ranking can use normalized discounted cumulative gain on a fixed set of judged candidates:

    DCG@K = Σi=1…K (2relevance_i − 1) / log2(i+1)
    NDCG@K = DCG@K / ideal DCG@K

    The denominator is the best ordering of those same candidates; a convention is needed when every relevance label is zero. NDCG rewards placing useful items early, but its interpretation depends on the labels and candidate pool. It does not directly measure user satisfaction.

    Writer comparisons should freeze source facts and selected events. Otherwise, an adapted writer given a more complete snapshot may appear better for a reason unrelated to adaptation. Include provider failures and template deliveries in product-level reporting, with quality broken down by route.

    The source material does not establish production factuality, trained-ranker uplift, cache-hit rate, or p95 latency. Those remain measurements to collect. The architecture identifies where to measure them; it does not supply absent results.

    Compute ranking quality on an actual ordered list

    Suppose three candidate items have graded relevance [3,0,2] in displayed order. Their gains under 2rel−1 are [7,0,3], giving DCG=7/1 + 0/log₂3 + 3/2 = 8.5. The ideal order is [3,2,0], with IDCG=7 + 3/log₂3≈8.893. NDCG is therefore about 0.956.

    The score identifies the cost of putting the irrelevant item ahead of a useful one. It says nothing about an important game missing from the candidate pool, so candidate recall needs its own measurement. Nor should labels derived from the existing heuristic be used to claim real-user preference gains over that heuristic.

    For generation, a more direct unit is a claim: subject, relation, value, time, and evidence. “Player B scored 30” can then be checked against player–stat pairs rather than a bag of allowed tokens. Report the share of supported claims, the share of important facts covered, and error severity. A writer that says almost nothing can have high precision while providing little product value.

    Find the bottleneck before adding workers

    At the final buzzer, many fans can request the same uncached game. More API workers create more application capacity, but also more independent local caches and potential calls to the same sports and model providers.

    These locks protect memo operations, not in-flight generation. A warm recap hit bypasses both fetching and writing; a source-cache hit may still require generation. Reporting one blended “cache latency” would hide the distinction.

    The async endpoint directly calls a synchronous recap function. Blocking network or model work inside that call can occupy the event loop. An async declaration does not automatically make its callees yield. A suitable thread path or durable job path, selected according to the task lifecycle, would isolate that blocking work.

    Diagnostics also need request scope. A module-global last-error value can be overwritten by another request before a fallback reads it. The displayed reason could then belong to a different game. Returning errors with the attempt result avoids this shared mutable state.

    Capacity planning starts with arrival rate and service time. In an illustrative stable system, two uncached jobs per second with ten seconds of average time in the system imply about twenty concurrent jobs by Little’s law. If a dependency can complete only one job per second, that workload cannot remain stable without reducing admitted work, deduplicating, or changing the service path.

    Measure same-game bursts separately from many-game bursts. The former exposes duplicated work; the latter exposes aggregate upstream capacity. Trace queueing, source fetching, selection, each provider attempt, and storage. Sequential fallback means the fan’s latency includes failed attempts as well as the successful one.

    Bounded concurrency, shared deadlines, jittered retries, and per-key coalescing are proposed improvements with direct relevance to this design. Buying inference hardware would address a different bottleneck unless measurement first shows that hosted generation is what needs replacing.

    Inference economics depend on reuse and fallback

    Let h be the full-recap cache-hit fraction, Chit the cost of serving a hit, and Cmiss the average cost of fetching and generating on a miss. A simple workload model is:

    E[C per request] = hChit + (1−h)Cmiss
    E[T] = hE[T|hit] + (1−h)E[T|miss]

    The latency identity concerns the mean, not p95. A small fraction of very slow misses can dominate the tail while barely changing a median. Increasing hit rate also helps only when the cached artifact remains valid for the source snapshot and personalization context.

    For a three-stage sequential writer chain, let q1 be the first attempt's failure probability and q2 the conditional failure probability of the second given that it is reached. Expected writer cost is C₁ + q₁C₂ + q₁q₂C₃ when each C is the appropriate conditional attempt cost. Latency must include the time spent on failed attempts, including timeout behavior.

    As a toy illustration, an 80% hit rate, one-millisecond hit, and ten-second average miss give a mean near two seconds. That attractive average still leaves one in five requests on the slow route. These are sizing examples, not production measurements. For a company serving fans around game endings, hit/miss mix, burst concurrency, and cost per completed experience are necessary context for any inference-speed claim.

    The technical decisions serve one fan experience

    Replays AI's company thesis is a personal sports desk that understands what a fan follows and fits the explanation into the time they have. The data and model architecture exists to deliver that experience consistently.

    The equations now make the responsibilities explicit. Logistic proxies encode assumptions about game state. The curation model scores relevance. Autoregressive inference turns selected evidence into language. Supervised adaptation changes the writer's behavior toward accepted targets. Cache and fallback policies determine how much repeated work the service performs.

    The next useful improvement depends on which stage limits the experience. Better data and event selection help when the final stop is missing. Better supervision helps when the writer misstates an available fact. Better batching or reuse helps when the right answer takes too long or costs too much. Those are different experiments even when the UI presents one continuous broadcast.

    The durable goal is not simply more generated sports content. It is a fan who can finish an experience, understand the game they cared about, and trust the important details. That is the outcome against which the inference and data choices should be judged.

    Both applications become easier to reason about when evidence, prediction, and presentation remain separate. A catalog-valid code still needs contextual support. A known number still needs the correct player. A schema-valid report still needs an audience and a review state.
