    Synthure: building around the work a hospital visit leaves unfinished

    Synthure began with conversations while I was volunteering at a hospital. Patients needed clearer explanations, staff needed better coordination, and people needed help finding care and coverage that fit what they were paying for. Those needs shape the system, from its shared encounter record to its models and review workflow.

    The conversations that led to Synthure

    While volunteering at a hospital, I asked people about their pain points. Three themes came up: patients did not always understand their care; administrative work suffered from poor communication; and people struggled to find the right provider and understand whether their coverage and spending fit their needs.

    These were connected problems. An explanation that a patient could not understand could become another question for the care team. Missing information could become another administrative handoff. Choosing a provider without understanding coverage and cost could leave someone with a decision that looked reasonable medically but made little sense financially.

    That was the motivation for Synthure. The question was broader than whether AI could summarize a clinical note. Could the information from an encounter become something useful to every person who needed to act on it, without making each person reconstruct the same story?

    The financial part of that question also goes beyond estimating a bill. A person wants care that fits their profile, a provider appropriate for their needs, and confidence that they are not paying more than necessary. A useful product has to connect clinical needs, coverage, provider suitability, and cost. A model that simply chooses the cheapest number would not solve that problem.

    The implementation examined here develops a foundation for that ambition: turn a messy note into a reviewable encounter record, connect that record to coding and reference data, and derive different views from the same state. Full personalized coverage and provider matching require additional data and validation; they are part of the larger product direction, rather than a capability established by the current code.

    This article starts with those hospital conversations and the product decisions they imply. The technical sections then follow the same questions: how to preserve what happened, how to identify what is missing, how to connect it to useful reference information, and how to keep every view consistent when someone makes a correction.

    Three needs, one underlying information problem

    Start with the patient. A note may be perfectly readable to a clinician and still fail as an explanation. The patient needs to understand what was found, which decisions remain open, what the next step is, and what information could change that step. Expanding abbreviations helps, but the more important task is preserving the distinction between a confirmed condition and a possibility.

    For example, “knee surgery is being considered” supports an explanation of a possible next step. It does not support instructions that assume surgery happened. This is why Synthure needs structured evidence before it needs polished language. The patient's experience depends on whether the software preserves status, uncertainty, and timing.

    Now consider the administrative team. A question such as “Is authorization on file?” has an owner, a state, and consequences for other people. If it is answered in one portal while another still shows it as unresolved, generating a better summary creates little value. The system needs a shared state transition that other views can observe.

    The provider-and-coverage problem is more demanding still. A diagnosis can help identify an appropriate kind of care, but it cannot determine plan eligibility, network participation, provider availability, a negotiated price, or whether a different option is a better fit. Those are separate inputs. Combining them responsibly requires explicit data joins and update policies, not asking a language model to infer missing insurance facts.

    What people neededWhat the product must representTechnical consequence
    Understand the encounterSupported facts, uncertainty, and next stepsSource spans, assertion status, and audience-specific explanation
    Coordinate administrative workWho changed what, what remains unresolved, and who needs to knowShared encounter state, actions, derived views, and traceable events
    Find appropriate care without unnecessary spendingPatient needs, coverage constraints, suitable providers, and comparable costsVerified eligibility and provider data, explicit assumptions, and a separately evaluated matching workflow

    This mapping determines the order of the engineering. First establish a reliable account of the encounter. Then connect the account to rules and reference data. Only then use it to support decisions and explanations. Beginning with a provider recommendation before those inputs exist would produce confidence faster than it produces knowledge.

    Why this is still hard when healthcare already has so much software

    There is no shortage of products for notes, coding, revenue-cycle work, or patient communication. Abridge describes encounter-grounded documentation and revenue-cycle capabilities, and Ambience exposes documentation and coding capabilities through APIs. These are examples of related offerings, not an exhaustive market comparison. Synthure's case cannot rest on a claim that nobody has attempted these problems.

    The product thesis is about continuity: preserve the meaning of the encounter as it moves between people, and let a verified change affect all the views that depend on it. That is a goal to demonstrate in a workflow, rather than an assertion of exclusive functionality.

    One obstacle is that each workflow asks a different question of the same note. A clinical phrase may be enough to explain a condition but insufficient to assign a specific code. A valid code may still leave missing documentation. Complete documentation does not establish coverage. Coverage does not establish that a particular provider is available or the best fit. These implications stop at different boundaries.

    Another obstacle is that the evidence changes at different rates. Clinical facts can be corrected during review. Plan benefits can change between coverage periods. Provider participation and availability need current sources. An explanation generated last week may be grammatically perfect while depending on an obsolete assumption.

    Access also differs by role. A shared internal representation does not imply that every reader should receive every field. The clinician's detailed view and an employer-facing aggregate view need different disclosure rules. Redacting an input is one useful operation; controlling what each downstream view can reveal is another.

    Finally, the feedback needed to learn each task is different. Mention-to-code examples can train a linker. They do not train a provider matcher or establish savings. A readiness label does not reveal whether a payer later accepted a claim. A company has to earn each of these claims with the appropriate data and outcome measurement.

    This explains the initial scope of Synthure: clinical-note normalization and a claim-readiness copilot. It is a concrete place to make the shared-information idea useful and testable. The broader matching ambition can build on that foundation when eligibility, provider, price, and outcome data are available.

    The first product decision: make the encounter the unit of work

    Before following the implementation, separate an ordinary rule from a learned model. A rule is written directly, such as “do not accept a code absent from the catalog.” A model is a calculation whose adjustable numbers were fitted using examples. Training chooses those numbers; inference uses the fixed numbers on a new note. Neither operation supplies facts absent from the input.

    The browser runs on the user's device. The server is the remote application coordinating requests and other services. An API specifies the request and response formats they use to communicate. We will follow the information across these boundaries rather than treat “AI” as one operation.

    Four separate AI summaries would reproduce the fragmentation that motivated the company. Each could reinterpret the same phrase, choose different details, and become stale after a correction. Synthure instead builds a shared record and uses it to support patient, physician, hospital, and employer views.

    The record begins with the note, but grows beyond text. An entity is a meaningful item found in the text, such as a condition, medication, or identifier. The record holds these items alongside possible medical codes, missing details, proposed services, and reference prices. Shared state means the current accepted version of this information, which several screens can read.

    The browser runs local redaction and biomedical extraction. The server adds lookups, compact-model predictions, and hosted narration. These are different kinds of computation. Extraction proposes what the note says; lookup connects it to a catalog; prediction estimates a labeled outcome; narration explains the resulting record.

    note → cleaned text → supported mentions → candidate codes and gaps
    → reviewable encounter → derived workflow state → audience-specific explanation

    That division is a response to the original pain points. Local processing limits raw-note transmission in the normal browser workflow. Constrained catalog selection narrows coding errors. A shared encounter makes a correction reusable. Audience-specific writers help with comprehension after the evidence has been assembled.

    The architecture creates a useful debugging question: where did meaning change? If every view incorrectly marks a proposed procedure as completed, inspect the common record. If only the patient explanation changes its status, inspect the writer. If a reviewed correction fails to reach another portal, inspect state propagation. The product problem gives each technical boundary a purpose.

    From an attractive prediction to a defensible task

    The repository records a revealing change in direction. An earlier version used note keywords to produce a denial-risk score. The README explains that this score was removed. The implemented direction separates documentation readiness and explicit checks from an unsupported probability of denial.

    Why does that matter? Predicting a denial would require a target such as the eventual outcome of a submitted claim, linked to the information available when the prediction was made. A model trained on generated documentation labels learns a different task. Calling its output a denial probability would not change the supervision it received.

    Readiness is narrower and more actionable. Is a required detail missing? Is the code valid? Is an authorization requirement unresolved in the record? These questions can lead to a specific review task. A clinician or coder can inspect the reason and supply the missing information.

    The current system contains both a learned documentation-readiness predictor and deterministic readiness checks. They should remain distinguishable. The predictor summarizes patterns in its training label; the checks expose individually inspectable conditions. Neither certifies coverage or establishes the outcome of an external claim.

    This evolution connects the company motivation to model design. The ambition is to reduce confusion and unnecessary work. A score contributes only if its meaning is clear enough for someone to use and challenge. Otherwise it creates another opaque artifact that staff must interpret.

    Following one note through the system

    We will use this note throughout: “Patient A has type 2 diabetes and right-knee osteoarthritis. No chest pain. Knee surgery is being considered. Medication details are incomplete; prior authorization status is unknown.”

    There are two positive conditions, a negated symptom, a possible future procedure, and unresolved documentation. Flattening this into a list of medical phrases would lose most of the distinctions that matter.

    The browser first loads its models, runs deidentify(note), and then runs extractEntities(deid.text). A model-loading or inference failure stops the normal browser path before the synthesis POST. Extracting after redaction matters because replacing a name with a placeholder can move every later character. Entity offsets must describe the same cleaned string the server receives.

    From token predictions to clinical assertions

    The first task is to find meaningful phrases without changing what they say. In “Alex reports right knee pain,” the system might locate a name and a condition. The name can be replaced before the note is sent onward; the condition becomes an item for review. Named-entity recognition, or NER, assigns these categories to pieces of text.

    A span is a continuous stretch of characters. A token is a piece used by the model: a word, part of a word, or punctuation. The model labels tokens, but the application needs spans that a person can highlight and verify.

    Try a threshold before looking inside the model. In this invented example, “Alex” receives a name score of 0.94 and “right knee pain” a condition score of 0.86. There is also an error: “reports” receives a condition score of 0.55. A threshold is the minimum score accepted. Raise it above 0.55 to remove the error; raise it above 0.86 and a useful condition disappears too.

    The slider changes an acceptance rule, not the model. A score of 0.86 does not automatically mean an 86% chance that the clinical assertion is correct. That interpretation would need calibration on suitable labeled examples. Here, inspect what the rule keeps and what it discards.

    What the entity model does with a sentence

    Now consider No chest pain. Right knee pain persists. A tokenizer converts text pieces into vocabulary IDs, like recording dictionary entry numbers. Those IDs are lookup keys: token 200 does not mean twice as much as token 100.

    Each ID becomes an ordered list of numbers. Such a list is a vector; a learned numerical representation is an embedding. The entries need not represent concepts we can name individually. Their purpose is to support useful transformations and comparisons. A transformer is a model architecture that lets a token's representation depend on other available tokens, so “pain” near “No” can acquire a different representation from “pain” near “persists.”

    A final classification head converts the contextual vector into a score for each allowed label. These unbounded scores are logits. Softmax turns them into positive shares adding up to one. Exponentiating means raising the constant e (approximately 2.718) to each score. For toy logits 2 and 1, e²≈7.39 and e¹≈2.72. Dividing each by their sum, 10.11, gives shares 0.731 and 0.269. This makes a larger logit receive a larger share.

    Optional notation: the token classifier

    i names a token position; hᵢ is its vector; d is the number of vector entries. W is a table of learned weights and b a list of starting scores. Their multiplication and addition produce label logits zᵢ. There are L possible labels. ℝ means real-valued numbers, ∈ means “belongs to,” and Σ means “add these terms.” The last expression is the exponentiate-and-divide softmax calculation above. Its pᵢ,ℓ is the share for label ℓ at token position i; the index j runs over all labels.

    hᵢ ∈ ℝᵈ → zᵢ = Whᵢ + b ∈ ℝᴸ → pᵢ,ℓ = exp(zᵢ,ℓ) / Σⱼ exp(zᵢ,ⱼ)

    B-DISEASE means “begin a disease mention”; I-DISEASE means “continue that mention”; O means “outside the recognized categories.” These labels locate text. They do not by themselves say whether a condition is present, absent, historical, or uncertain.

    The application still has work after the model returns labels. It must merge compatible pieces, locate the character range in the cleaned string, and decide whether the span clears the threshold. If the three pieces of a mention have scores 0.96, 0.87, and 0.92, the inspected minimum-score aggregation returns 0.87. It neither multiplies the scores nor estimates the probability that the complete clinical assertion is correct. The predictions share context and should not be assumed independent.

    Most importantly, locating “chest pain” is different from asserting that the patient has it. An extractor can correctly highlight both pain mentions while a downstream system incorrectly treats both as present. A useful record needs the mention, its source range, and its assertion status. That separation explains why patient comprehension and administrative accuracy depend on more than a high entity-detection score.

    What int8 changes, and what it leaves intact

    Quantization stores some model numbers with fewer bits, rather like recording a measurement to fewer decimal places. With step size 0.01, weight 0.123 becomes 0.123/0.01=12.3, rounds to integer 12, and reconstructs as 12×0.01=0.12. Storage is smaller, but the reconstructed value is slightly different.

    A general version includes a zero point, an integer representing real zero. It writes q=clamp(round(w/s)+z₀) and reconstructs ŵ=s(q−z₀). Here w is the original weight, s the step size, z₀ the zero point, q the saved integer, and ŵ the reconstructed weight. “Clamp” keeps an integer within the supported range. This explains the idea rather than specifying every operation in the exported models.

    One million int8 weight entries occupy approximately one megabyte of payload, compared with four megabytes for float32 entries. Scale metadata, intermediate activations, downloads, and runtime buffers add other costs. The browser's int8 models therefore reduce a particular storage component; they do not imply a fourfold end-to-end speedup or establish identical extraction quality.

    BIO tags mark the beginning and continuation of an entity. The aggregation code joins compatible pieces, removes WordPiece’s ## marker, and searches forward for the reconstructed phrase. It assigns the minimum token score to a merged span. This conservative aggregation prevents a weak token from being hidden by an average, although it is not a calibrated probability of correct clinical interpretation.

    A span is saved using character positions. In [start,end), the start is included and the end excluded: [5,10) covers five characters, positions 5 through 9. Replacing those five characters with twelve moves every later character seven places. Replacing the last span first avoids moving any earlier span that still needs editing.

    Why some decisions fit small models

    Identifying a note category or checking for missing documentation is a narrower task than writing a patient explanation. Synthure serves these decisions using exported weights in TypeScript. This avoids a hosted generation call and makes the computation inspectable.

    Build the feature vector by hand

    We need numbers before a small classifier can score a note. A feature is a measurable property used in that calculation. Here the features are counts of terms. Use a tiny vocabulary: pain, knee, and knee pain. A single word is a unigram; an adjacent pair is a bigram. “Knee pain; pain persists” has counts [2,1,1]. This ordered list is the feature vector, and its positions must keep the same meaning.

    TF-IDF combines term frequency—how often a term occurs in this note—with inverse document frequency—how rare it was across the training documents. Assume saved IDF weights [1.0,1.5,2.0]. These are illustrative values; inference loads the trained weights rather than fitting new ones from this note.

    First soften repeated occurrences with 1+ln(count). The natural logarithm, written ln, grows more slowly than the count: ln(1)=0 and ln(2)≈0.693. Two appearances therefore receive weight 1.693 before IDF rather than twice the weight of one. Multiply by each feature's own IDF to obtain [1.693,1.500,2.000].

    Next put the vector on a common scale. Square each entry, add the squares, and take the square root. This gives its L2 length, about 3.019. Divide every entry by this same length to get [0.561,0.497,0.662]. This normalization preserves feature proportions while reducing the effect of overall magnitude. We call the unnormalized vector v and the normalized vector x. The symbol ‖v‖₂ below means exactly this length.

    counts = [2, 1, 1]
    v = [(1+ln 2)×1, (1+ln 1)×1.5, (1+ln 1)×2]
    v ≈ [1.693, 1.500, 2.000]
    ‖v‖₂ = √(1.693² + 1.500² + 2.000²) ≈ 3.019
    x = v/‖v‖₂ ≈ [0.561, 0.497, 0.662]

    A possible note category, or class, has one learned coefficient per feature. With toy coefficients [0.4,1.0,0.8], multiply corresponding entries: 0.4×0.561=0.224, 1.0×0.497=0.497, and 0.8×0.662=0.530. Adding gives about 1.251. Multiplying corresponding entries and adding them is called a dot product.

    The class also has an intercept, a starting score before the feature contributions. An intercept of −0.3 gives −0.3+1.251≈0.951. This is a logit, not yet a probability. Another class has different weights and an intercept, so the same input produces another logit.

    This is why “the model predicts a note type” can be made inspectable: each multiplication contributes an amount to the final logit. The chart below plots those amounts for two toy classes. It uses a two-class softmax for clarity; the real classifier uses its exported class set.

    Softmax exponentiates the class logits and divides by their total. In this chart, class A has logit 0.951 and class B about 0.201, yielding two-class shares of approximately 67.9% and 32.1%. The real classifier uses its full exported class set. Increasing “pain” changes one raw count and also the common normalization length, so the other contributions change too.

    A useful debugging sequence is now explicit: inspect token counts, then vocabulary indices, then IDF-weighted values, then the normalized vector, then each class logit. Checking only the final label can hide an indexing error or a preprocessing difference between Python training and TypeScript serving.

    Note type: TF-IDF and a linear classifier

    The implementation performs the same sequence: count recognized terms, apply saved IDF weights, normalize, multiply by each class's coefficients, and compare the resulting logits. The compact notation below summarizes that arithmetic. You do not need to memorize it to follow the system.

    Optional notation: dot products and stable softmax

    x is the feature vector; j names a class; wⱼ contains its weights; bⱼ is its intercept. Superscript T turns a column into a row so that wⱼᵀx denotes the dot product. zⱼ is the logit and pⱼ its softmax share. The index k runs over all classes. “max z” is the largest logit. Subtracting it keeps exponentials numerically manageable without changing the shares.

    zj = bj + wjTx
    pj = exp(zj − max z) / Σk exp(zk − max z)

    The subtraction matters because exponentials grow quickly. For logits 1,002 and 1,001, subtracting the largest produces 0 and −1. The relative odds remain the same while intermediate numbers become small enough to represent reliably.

    Missing fields: several labels can be true

    Missing-field detection uses another list of features: counts, yes/no indicators, note length, and note type. A yes/no indicator is 1 or 0. A one-hot note-type encoding has one position per possible category and sets only the selected category's position to 1.

    Each missing-field question receives its own logit. Sigmoid maps one logit to a value between zero and one: σ(z)=1/(1+e^(−z)). Here z is the logit, e the constant approximately 2.718, and σ the function's name. Logit 0 maps to 0.5, logit 1 to about 0.731, and −1 to about 0.269. Separate sigmoids do not have to add up to one. Dosage and laterality can both be missing, so these labels should not be forced to compete.

    The returned present flag means the missing-field prediction exceeded 0.5. It does not mean the medical detail was supplied. There is also a representational limit: “Left arm examined; knee pain” contains a side, but not the knee’s side. Numerical parity with training cannot repair a feature representation that loses this association.

    Inference is the execution of a learned decision rule

    Training chooses the vocabulary, IDF values, coefficients, and intercepts from examples. Inference holds those artifacts fixed and calculates one new note's scores. “Exported weights” means the learned numbers were saved in a format the TypeScript application can read, allowing it to execute the calculation without asking a hosted writer.

    With C classes and m nonzero features, scoring does roughly C×m multiply-and-add operations after tokenization. O(Cm) describes how work grows, not a measured response time. Skipping zero features is called sparse computation.

    That makes these models suitable for frequent, narrow decisions in the workflow. It also makes model versioning inseparable from preprocessing versioning. Deploying new coefficients with an old vocabulary changes the meaning of every multiplication. A useful export test compares intermediate feature vectors and logits between training and serving, before softmax or rounding can hide discrepancies.

    Retrieving a code is easier than establishing that it applies

    The next task connects a supported phrase to an entry in a medical-code catalog. Retrieval means finding plausible entries; ranking puts those candidates in order. Two code families appear here: ICD codes identify diagnoses, and CPT codes identify procedures or services. Their descriptions and detailed rules matter; they are more specific than free-form medical phrases. Restricting the output to catalog entries prevents invented identifiers, but a real identifier can still be wrong for the note. Abstention means declining to return a code when the available match is insufficient.

    Follow the inverted index through one lookup

    An inverted index reverses a catalog. Instead of asking each description whether it contains “knee,” it stores the list of descriptions containing “knee” once. Consider a toy catalog with three descriptions: A, “right knee osteoarthritis”; B, “left knee osteoarthritis”; and C, “right shoulder osteoarthritis.”

    TokenStored candidate IDs
    rightA, C
    kneeA, B
    osteoarthritisA, B, C

    Intersecting all three lists for “right knee osteoarthritis” leaves A. For “knee osteoarthritis,” intersection leaves A and B. No ranking score can recover a side that was never specified. Relaxing token requirements helps when the catalog uses different wording, but also admits candidates with conflicting qualifiers.

    The local reranker converts each surviving candidate into four features and computes a logistic score. It is a second decision over a small set, rather than a second search over the entire catalog. The acceptance floor then decides whether to return the best candidate at all. Retrieval, ranking, and abstention respectively answer “what is available?”, “which is best?”, and “is the evidence sufficient to use it?”

    Retrieving a code and retrieving evidence for a writer also serve different purposes. The first links a mention to a controlled vocabulary. Retrieval-augmented generation supplies selected source material inside a writer's context. Both can use vector search, but a good code match does not by itself supply a payer policy or prove that a proposed service is covered.

    The lexical path uses an inverted index: tokens point to catalog terms containing them. icdCandidates expands abbreviations, tries all unique query tokens, relaxes to one fewer, and finally backs off to strong tokens. Ordering accounts for overlap, agreement with the head word, and extra words in the candidate.

    The route requests up to eight candidates. A logistic reranker scores overlap, term length, billability, and retrieval rank; the best candidate must clear 0.15. Codes are then checked against the reference and deduplicated.

    const LINK_FLOOR = 0.15 // reranker probability below which we abstain

function linkDiagnoses(diagnoses: Entity[]): LinkedCode[] {
  const out: LinkedCode[] = []
  const usedCodes = new Set<string>()
  for (const e of diagnoses) {
    const cands = icdCandidates(e.text, 8)
    if (!cands.length) continue
    let best: { c: IcdCandidate; s: number } | null = null
    for (const c of cands) {
      const s = rerankScore({ overlap: c.overlap, termlen: c.termLen, billable: c.billable ? 1 : 0, rank: c.rank })
      if (!best || s > best.s) best = { c, s }
    }
    if (!best || best.s < LINK_FLOOR) continue // abstain -> entity stays uncoded
    const key = best.c.code.replace('.', '').toUpperCase()
    if (usedCodes.has(key)) continue
    usedCodes.add(key)
    out.push({ code: best.c.code, label: best.c.description, billable: best.c.billable, source: 'linked', entity: e.text, score: Number(best.s.toFixed(3)) })
  }
  return out

    Learning similarity when words do not overlap

    A word-based search can miss phrases that mean the same thing using different words. The optional neural coder turns both the note phrase and catalog descriptions into embeddings: learned lists of numbers intended to make useful matches numerically similar. Similarity is a comparison score, not a medical conclusion.

    From token vectors to a searchable catalog

    An encoder is a model that creates vectors. Suppose it returns four rows of three numbers. This rectangular table is a matrix with shape 4×3: one row per token position and three features per row. If the fourth row is padding used to make inputs equally long, a mask says to ignore it.

    To make one vector for the phrase, average each column over the real rows. With rows [1,0,1], [1,1,0], and [0,1,1], every column sums to 2. Divide by three real rows to get [2/3,2/3,2/3]. This is mean pooling. Divide that vector by its L2 length to get [0.577,0.577,0.577], a unit vector whose length is one.

    The shorthand is u=Σᵢmᵢhᵢ/Σᵢmᵢ. Here hᵢ is row i; mᵢ is 1 for a real token or 0 for padding; Σ means to add. The numerator adds retained rows, and the denominator counts them. It is the same column-by-column average just calculated.

    The catalog stores one such vector per description. With C codes and embedding width d, its matrix has shape C×d; multiplying it by a single query vector produces C similarity scores. This is the basic computation behind exhaustive dense retrieval. Approximate indexes change how candidates are found, but are not necessary to understand the score.

    Separately encoding each description makes caching possible. Jointly encoding a mention and description allows their tokens to interact before scoring, but that computation depends on the particular pair and cannot be fully precomputed for every future mention.

    Why contrastive loss changes the embedding space

    How does the encoder learn useful similarities? Give it a known matching phrase and description alongside competing descriptions. A loss measures how poorly it performs on this training task; training tries to lower it. In this contrastive task, the correct pair should receive a larger share than competitors.

    Work through two candidates with similarities 0.8 and 0.2, where the first is correct. Divide by a chosen temperature of 0.2 to get logits 4 and 1. Exponentiating gives approximately 54.60 and 2.72. The correct share is 54.60/(54.60+2.72)≈0.953. Applying −ln(share) gives loss about 0.049. Equal similarities would give share 0.5 and the larger loss 0.693.

    The negative logarithm makes a confidently missed positive expensive: a correct-pair share of 0.01 gives loss about 4.605. Temperature changes how sharply differences affect shares. It does not add evidence about whether a code applies. These shares describe competition within the batch, not calibrated clinical probabilities.

    A gradient describes how a small adjustment would change the loss. Gradient descent adjusts learned numbers in a direction that reduces the loss. In this example, the training signal pushes the positive similarity upward and competing similarities downward. Backpropagation carries that signal backward through the encoder to its adjustable weights.

    The competitors are called negatives, but two mentions may share the same correct code. Treating one valid A description as the other mention's wrong answer would punish a legitimate synonym. In this chart, rows are mentions and columns are descriptions. The outlined diagonal cells mark assigned matching pairs. Toggle masking to remove the other same-code A column from the competition.

    The diagonal outlines mark the designated positive pairs. The duplicated A descriptions are valid matches even though only one occupies each diagonal position. Masking the other same-code column removes it from the competition. The displayed loss is the average row cross-entropy; the source additionally averages the reverse, description-to-mention direction. Both directions depend on the examples that share a batch.

    A bi-encoder encodes the mention and description separately. The application can therefore calculate catalog vectors in advance. For a new note, it creates the query vector and compares it with saved descriptions. For unit vectors, their dot product equals cosine similarity: 1 means the same direction, 0 perpendicular directions, and −1 opposite directions. These are directions in learned feature space, not physical directions.

    A cross-encoder reads the mention and candidate description together. Their words can interact before it assigns a score, but a new pair requires another model calculation. A common design therefore retrieves a broad set cheaply and then scores a small shortlist jointly.

    The training objective

    The implementation stores all comparisons in a square score table. Each row is one mention, each column one description, and designated matches occupy the diagonal. The row and column indices match there; the text need not be identical. The source averages the loss in both directions: mentions finding descriptions and descriptions finding mentions.

    Optional notation: average contrastive loss

    A batch contains B training pairs. The row index i names a mention; column j names a description. Sᵢⱼ is their dot product divided by temperature; Sᵢᵢ is the assigned positive score. The fraction is its softmax share among allowed columns. The minus-log operation penalizes small positive shares, and division by B averages the row losses. This is the multi-row version of the two-candidate calculation above.

    Lmention→code = −(1/B) Σi log [exp(Sii) / Σj allowed exp(Sij)]

    Usually, off-diagonal examples serve as negatives. But two mentions in a batch may map to the same code. Penalizing those pairs would teach the model to separate legitimate synonyms. This implementation masks same-code collisions and averages cross-entropy in both retrieval directions:

    def info_nce(anchor: torch.Tensor, positive: torch.Tensor, codes: torch.Tensor, temperature: float) -> torch.Tensor:
    """Symmetric in-batch InfoNCE.

    anchor[i] and positive[i] are the phrase and its code's description. Any j!=i
    with the same code is masked out so a true synonym is never punished as a
    negative (in-batch collisions are common in a 98k-code space).
    """

    same = codes.unsqueeze(0) == codes.unsqueeze(1)         # (B, B) same code

    Turning a model score into a review policy

    Our example still has incomplete medication details and an unknown authorization status. A readiness predictor can help prioritize review, but cannot turn those unknowns into facts. Its job is to estimate a labeled documentation outcome from the features available.

    Trace a tree before interpreting its probability

    A decision tree is a series of questions about an input. An illustrative first question asks whether diagnosis count is zero. The answer chooses a left or right branch, perhaps leading to another question. Eventually the calculation reaches a leaf, a stored numerical contribution. One note visits one leaf in each tree, rather than averaging all the possible leaves.

    Gradient boosting combines many trees. Training adds trees that improve the current predictions; inference follows their stored questions and adds the chosen leaf contributions. Positive contributions raise the logit, negative ones lower it. A learning-rate multiplier sets the size of each contribution. Sigmoid then converts the final logit into a score between zero and one.

    Calibration is a separate mapping that tries to make score values agree with observed frequencies. A model can rank easier cases above harder ones while being overconfident about both. Calibration changes that interpretation; it does not supply a missing fact.

    Suppose a raw score of 0.5 maps to calibrated 0.4. The intention is that comparable cases assigned about 0.4 meet the labeled outcome about 40% of the time. It is not a claim that this note is “40% complete.” Nor does the label describe payer approval unless that is actually the outcome used in training.

    The vertical line is a decision policy; the points are model outputs. Moving the line does not retrain the model. It changes which cases enter the accepted set. With a small sample, one case can sharply change the displayed error rate, so evaluation needs counts as well as percentages. In Synthure, this statistical decision also remains subordinate to deterministic blocking findings.

    The saved predictor repeats this calculation: start with a bias, follow each tree to a leaf, scale the leaf value, and add the contributions. The bias is the starting logit. The compact equation below is another way to write these instructions.

    Optional notation: adding the trees

    x is the feature list; m numbers the trees; leafₘ(x) is the reached leaf value. Σ adds the values. The Greek η (eta) is the learning-rate multiplier, b the starting logit, and σ the sigmoid function. praw is the score before calibration.

    praw = σ(b + η Σm leafm(x))

    Take bias −0.4, learning rate 0.1, and leaf values 1.2 and −0.3. First add the leaves: 1.2−0.3=0.9. Scale: 0.1×0.9=0.09. Add the bias: −0.4+0.09=−0.31. Sigmoid gives 1/(1+e^0.31)≈0.423. This is a result of learned contributions, not the fraction of documentation rules that passed.

    function gbmProb(x: number[]): number {
  const gbm = (readinessModel as { gbm: { trees: Tree[]; learning_rate: number; init: number } }).gbm
  let s = gbm.init
  for (const t of gbm.trees) {
    let node = 0
    while (t.children_left[node] !== -1) {
      node = x[t.feature[node]] <= t.threshold[node] ? t.children_left[node] : t.children_right[node]
    }
    s += gbm.learning_rate * t.value[node]
  }
  return sigmoid(s)
}
function calibrate(p: number): number {
  const { x, y } = (readinessModel as { calibration: { x: number[]; y: number[] } }).calibration
  for (let i = 0; i < x.length - 1; i++) {
    if (p <= x[i + 1]) {
      const t = (p - x[i]) / (x[i + 1] - x[i] + 1e-9)
      return y[i] + t * (y[i + 1] - y[i])
    }
  }
  return y[y.length - 1]
}

    Make financial arithmetic visible

    The simplified estimator asks three questions: how much of the estimated service amount falls under the remaining deductible, what fraction of the rest goes to the patient, and does that exceed the remaining out-of-pocket limit? In this calculation, the deductible is the amount paid before the coinsurance step. Coinsurance is the patient's fraction of what remains. These inputs do not represent every rule in an insurance contract.

    Let A be the total priced-service estimate, D the remaining deductible, c the coinsurance fraction, and O the remaining out-of-pocket maximum. min(a,b) chooses the smaller number. Lowercase d is the deductible portion used for this encounter, capped at its estimate. “Round” rounds the final amount in the code's currency units.

    d = min(D, A)
    estimated patient amount = round(min(O, d + c(A−d)))

    Use services estimated at $1,000, deductible remaining $300, coinsurance 20%, and remaining maximum $800. The deductible portion is the smaller of $300 and $1,000: $300. This leaves $1,000−$300=$700. Twenty percent means 20/100=0.20, so coinsurance is 0.20×$700=$140. Add them: $300+$140=$440. The $800 cap does not change $440. A remaining cap of $400 would reduce it to $400. These are illustrative calculations under assumptions, not patient quotes.

    The code builds service estimates from reference prices and a selected payer multiplier. Unpriced services are excluded and surfaced as assumptions. Rounded line-item allocations can also differ slightly from the rounded total. These choices are important because a displayed total can otherwise look more complete and precise than its inputs.

    The internal “at risk” amount sums priced services with an unresolved authorization condition or no accepted supporting diagnosis. A local hash chain can help detect alterations only when a trusted earlier fingerprint survives independently; rewriting both records and hashes defeats that protection. Durable storage and anchored history would be separate engineering additions.

    What the evaluations actually measure

    The repository’s evaluation ledger is most useful when read as a set of component experiments. The dataset and denominator are part of every result. Combining unlike tests into one impression of “system accuracy” would lose the information needed to improve the system.

    Top-k accuracy asks whether the correct answer appears in the first k results. Mean reciprocal rank also rewards earlier placement: ranks 1, 2, and 5 contribute 1, 0.5, and 0.2. A miss contributes zero under the usual convention. Retrieval coverage and ordering should be examined separately.

    For extraction, begin with human-labeled supported mentions. A true positive (TP) is a real mention the system finds. A false positive (FP) is an incorrect mention it adds. A false negative (FN) is a real mention it misses.

    Precision asks, “Of the returned mentions, how many were right?” Recall asks, “Of the real mentions, how many were found?” If twelve real mentions exist, eight are found, and two incorrect ones are added, TP=8, FP=2, and FN=4. Precision is 8/(8+2)=0.80; recall is 8/(8+4)≈0.667. Their denominators answer different questions.

    F1 combines the two with a harmonic mean: multiply precision and recall, double the result, and divide by their sum. Here 2×0.80×0.667/(0.80+0.667)≈0.727. It falls when either component is low. It does not express how serious a particular mistake is.

    precision = TP/(TP+FP)
    recall = TP/(TP+FN)
    F1 = 2 × precision × recall / (precision + recall)

    Add false positives while leaving true positives fixed: precision falls because more returned mentions are wrong. Add false negatives instead: recall falls because more real mentions were missed. Counts make the distinction easier to interpret than one overall “accuracy” number.

    Two other measures appear below. AUROC measures ordering across thresholds: roughly, whether a randomly chosen positive case receives a higher score than a negative case. It does not establish that 0.8 means an 80% chance. Expected calibration error (ECE) groups predictions into score ranges and averages gaps between confidence and observed outcomes, weighted by group size. Its value depends on the grouping and outcome definition; lower is generally better under the same setup.

    Streaming changes when we see the answer

    A dependency means one operation needs another's result: an audit needs finished drafts. Streaming sends results as they become available, rather than holding everything until the request ends. A patient draft can appear while another writer is still working. This improves time to first visible output; final acceptance still waits for the required checks.

    Reconstruct one event from arbitrary network chunks

    The network transports bytes, not application objects. Imagine the server writes data: {"type":"report"} . One read might end after "rep; the next contains ort"} . Parsing the first chunk immediately would fail even though the server sent valid data.

    The decoder turns bytes into characters, preserving an incomplete multibyte character until the next read. A separate text buffer—accumulated text held in memory—preserves an incomplete event. Only once the double newline arrives does the client extract a full frame and parse its JSON, the structured-text format used for the event.

    A loop handles several events arriving in one read: it removes complete frames one at a time and retains any unfinished remainder. The two buffers solve distinct problems, bytes into characters and characters into events. Neither should be skipped because one simple test happened to deliver one full event per read.

    Finally, a run identifier decides whether the parsed event still belongs to the active interaction. Consider run A starting, run B replacing it, and A finishing late. Rejecting A's event prevents an old report from overwriting B's display. Cancellation is an additional operation that attempts to stop A's remaining work. Correct presentation and efficient resource use require both concepts, even though a user sees only one progress indicator.

    The client correctly treats transport chunks and application events as different units. A read can contain half an event, several events, or part of a multibyte character. A streaming decoder accumulates text, and the parser consumes complete double-newline-delimited frames:

    The animation introduces a separate completion issue. Deferred promises wait for both a minimum display interval and a stage event.     For performance work, separate cold model download, warm extraction, retrieval, writer fan-out, audit, and revision. Measure first-report latency, final-gate latency, provider failures, and cost per accepted run. A single elapsed-time average would obscure the stage responsible for the wait.

    The company goal is continuity from understanding to action

    The hospital conversations gave Synthure three connected goals: help patients understand, reduce administrative friction and communication gaps, and help people find care and coverage appropriate to their needs and spending. The shared encounter is the technical link between those goals.

    Extraction and coding make the note usable. Readiness checks make missing information actionable. Derived state makes corrections propagate. Audience-specific writing makes the result understandable. The provider-matching direction would add verified external options and an explicit decision process on top of that foundation.

    The current implementation provides evidence about component behavior and a prototype workflow, not proof of hospital-wide time savings or reduced patient spending. Those outcomes would need a pilot measuring review effort, repeated clarification, accepted-output error, and the quality of real care-and-cost decisions. Model accuracy matters because of its effect on those outcomes.

    The next version should therefore preserve the founding question through its engineering: when a person finishes using Synthure, do they understand more, does the next person have the information needed to act, and are important decisions supported by evidence they can inspect?
