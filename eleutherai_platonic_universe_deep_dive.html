    Platonic Universe, from galaxy images to a scientific test

    Start with what a telescope records. Build up to embeddings and similarity one calculation at a time, then understand why shared geometry does not guarantee shared physical predictions.

    How to study these notes

    Read the question at the start of each chapter, then follow the example. Pause at the “Remember” box and explain it in your own words. Formulas come after the idea; examples marked “illustration” are invented for learning, not recorded results.

    Note 01

    The research question, before any mathematics

    Question: If two models organize galaxies similarly, can a scientific predictor use their outputs interchangeably?

    Imagine observing the same galaxies with two instruments. One produces optical images; another produces infrared images. Both observations concern the same objects, but they record different wavelengths with different resolution and noise. The study asks whether trained models extract similar structure from these views—and whether that resemblance is scientifically useful in a stricter sense.

    There are two separate questions. Geometric agreement asks whether the models put the same galaxies near one another in their numerical descriptions. Scientific transfer asks whether a predictor fitted using one survey’s descriptions can predict a physical quantity from another survey’s descriptions without changing its settings.

    Illustration

    Suppose both views group galaxies A, B and C together. That is agreement about similarity. It does not yet show that a formula trained on the optical descriptions can recover redshift from the infrared descriptions. The formula depends on the meaning and scale of the individual numerical coordinates, not only on which objects are neighbors.

    The paper’s main conclusion is that detectable shared geometry is widespread in the tested representations, but it is insufficient to guarantee unchanged linear prediction across surveys. That conclusion is narrower than “models contain no astronomy” or “transfer can never work.” A different predictor or a learned conversion could answer a different question.

    Reading map

    First understand observations, models and numerical descriptions. Then learn how cosine similarity produces neighbor lists. Only after that compare geometry, fit a physical predictor and interpret the study’s results.

    Shared Geometry Is Not Shared Physics, the layerwise astronomy study. The abstract was rechecked for this rewrite; detailed numerical results below are retained from the original paper-linked guide.

    Note 02

    What a galaxy observation actually contains

    Question: What goes into the model?

    An image is a grid of measured values. A pixel stores information for one position in that grid. A channel stores a separate measurement at each position, such as one wavelength band or a processed color channel. A displayed color image is a representation of measurements; its red channel is not automatically a direct measure of a galaxy’s redshift.

    A spectrum records how much light is observed at different wavelengths. It is a different kind of input from a spatial image. A survey is a systematic observing program with its own instruments, processing and selection rules. HSC is an optical imaging reference in this study; DESI supplies spectra; Legacy Survey and JWST-related datasets provide additional views.

    Redshift describes how spectral wavelengths are stretched relative to reference wavelengths. It is related to cosmic expansion and motion, but the study treats catalog redshift as a target to predict, not as a number that can be read directly from an arbitrary image feature.

    Other targets include color, magnitude, size, stellar mass and specific star-formation rate. Color compares measurements in bands. Magnitude is a logarithmic brightness measure with smaller values indicating brighter sources. A half-light radius describes the radius enclosing half the measured light. Specific star-formation rate expresses star formation relative to stellar mass.

    Why two views of one galaxy differ

    Different filters admit different wavelengths. Resolution changes which small structures are visible. Noise adds measurement variation. The point-spread function describes how the instrument spreads light from a compact source. Calibration changes numerical scales. Selection rules determine which objects enter a catalog.

    Together these differences produce domain shift: the statistical properties of the input change between the setting where a method is fitted and the setting where it is used. Correctly matching the same galaxy removes an identity ambiguity; it does not remove domain shift.

    Remember

    Same galaxy does not mean same measured information. A cross-survey comparison needs both reliable object matching and awareness of how the observations differ.

    Note 03

    From pixels to a list of numbers: models and embeddings

    Question: How does an image become a numerical description?

    A model is a sequence of calculations with adjustable numerical settings called weights. During training, an objective is used to change those settings. During inference, an input passes through the already trained calculations. The study primarily runs pretrained models and records their internal outputs; it does not train 36 foundation models from scratch.

    A vector is simply an ordered list of numbers. The list [0.2, −0.8, 1.5] has three entries, so it has three dimensions. An embedding is a vector used to represent an input. Each entry is a learned numerical feature; it is not necessarily a named measurement such as mass or brightness.

    Walk through an illustrative vision transformer

    1. Prepare the image. Resize and scale values according to the model’s expected preprocessing. Suppose the input has three channels and is 224 × 224 pixels. Those choices are illustrative, not universal across the study.
    2. Divide it into patches. A vision transformer can split it into 16 × 16 pixel pieces. There are 14 patches along each side, hence 196 patches.
    3. Turn each patch into numbers. A learned calculation converts each patch’s pixel values into a token vector. A token here is a numerical description of an image patch, not a word.
    4. Mix information across patches. Repeated transformer blocks update those token vectors. Attention calculates how information from other tokens contributes; additional transformations modify each token’s features.
    5. Save a stage. A stage might output 197 token vectors, each with 768 entries, if the model includes one extra summary token.
    6. Reduce to one vector per image. A defined pooling rule averages token features or selects a designated summary token. This gives one 768-number description for the galaxy.

    Pooling means summarizing many vectors into fewer vectors. Averaging patch tokens and selecting a summary token are different choices and can produce different geometry. Record which choice was used.

    A matrix stacks vectors into rows. If 1,000 galaxies each have 768 features, the matrix has shape (1000, 768). Row 17 must refer to the same galaxy wherever matched comparisons rely on row 17. A tensor is the more general name for an array with any number of dimensions, such as a batch of images or a collection of token vectors.

    Illustrative shapes
    16 images:                 (16, 3, 224, 224)
Tokens after a block:       (16, 197, 768)
One vector per image:       (16, 768)
All saved galaxy vectors:  (number of galaxies, 768)

    The model’s “answer” in this experiment is those numerical arrays. Scientists then calculate similarities and fit small predictors using the arrays. The vision model is not being asked to explain a galaxy in a chat response.

    Remember

    An embedding is a learned description, not a table of automatically named physical properties. Useful physical meaning must be tested rather than assigned to an entry by intuition.

    Note 04

    Cosine similarity: what “direction” means for a list

    Question: How can two lists point in the same direction?

    Start with two numbers. Draw horizontal and vertical axes, then interpret [1, 2] as an arrow from zero to a point one unit across and two units up. [2, 4] reaches twice as far along the same line. The ratio between its entries is unchanged: the second entry is twice the first in both lists.

    That is the concrete meaning of the same direction: one nonzero vector is a positive multiple of the other. With hundreds of entries, drawing the arrow is impossible, but the same calculation still works. “Direction” refers to the relative pattern across coordinates, not a direction the galaxy points in the sky.

    Why remove length?

    The overall size of an embedding can vary. Cosine similarity compares the relative pattern after removing overall magnitude. This is appropriate when that is the relationship being studied. It deliberately discards length information, which might matter for another task.

    The length, also called the Euclidean norm, is found by squaring the entries, adding them and taking the square root. For a = [1, 2], the length is √(1² + 2²) = √5. For b = [2, 4], it is √20.

    The dot product multiplies corresponding entries and adds the products. Here it is 1 × 2 + 2 × 4 = 10. Divide that result by both lengths:

    cosine(a, b) = (a · b) / (‖a‖ × ‖b‖)
    = 10 / (√5 × √20)
    = 10 / 10 = 1

    a and b are the two vectors. The dot symbol means the dot product. Double bars mean vector length. The result is 1 because their directions match exactly, despite different lengths.

    For a = [1, 0] and b = [0, 1], the dot product is zero and both lengths are one. Their cosine is 0: the arrows are perpendicular. For [1, 0] and [−1, 0], the result is −1: opposite directions. A score of 0 means no directional alignment under this calculation, not a universal claim that the galaxies share no physical features.

    Normalization makes the comparison easier

    Divide every entry by the vector’s length. The resulting vector has length one while keeping its direction. This is row normalization when performed on every galaxy’s row. The dot product of two normalized rows is their cosine similarity. A zero vector has no direction; its cosine is undefined and must be handled explicitly.

    Try the two-number calculation

    Illustrative vectors only. Change either pair; no data leave this page.

    The important coordinate-system restriction

    Within one saved representation, the coordinates have a consistent interpretation, so comparing galaxy A and galaxy B by cosine is meaningful as a model-relative similarity. Two different models may use different widths or coordinate bases. Directly comparing entry 1 of one model with entry 1 of another is not automatically meaningful.

    The neighbor-overlap method avoids that problem: compute similarities within each view, then compare the resulting galaxy IDs. The next chapter explains why that is a more structural question.

    Remember

    Cosine compares the pattern of feature values after removing length. High cosine means similarity in the chosen learned representation, not proof of identical mass, redshift or other physical properties.

    Note 05

    Nearest neighbors and MKNN, calculated by hand

    Question: Do the two views agree about which other galaxies resemble each galaxy?

    For one galaxy, compare its embedding with every other galaxy in the same view. Sort by cosine similarity and retain the first k results. These are its k nearest neighbors. “Nearest” here is defined by this similarity rule; it does not mean spatially closest in the universe.

    Exclude the galaxy itself. Its self-similarity would otherwise usually be the largest value and create a trivial match. Repeat independently for the other survey, then compare neighbor identities for each matched galaxy.

    Illustration with six galaxies A–F and k = 2

    In the optical representation, A’s neighbors are {B, C}. In the infrared representation, A’s neighbors are {B, E}. The common set contains one galaxy, B. A’s overlap fraction is therefore 1/2 = 0.5.

    Repeat for all six galaxies. Suppose the average overlap is also 0.5. That average is the raw neighborhood-overlap score.

    The original guide calls this MKNN, mutual k-nearest-neighbor overlap. “Mutual” here describes agreement between views; do not confuse it with a separate graph convention that requires two objects to be each other’s neighbor within one view.

    Why chance correction is necessary

    With only six galaxies, A has five possible neighbors. Choosing two in each view creates overlap even when the choices are unrelated. The expected overlap fraction under the simple random-neighborhood baseline is k / (n − 1), where n is the total number of matched galaxies.

    chance fraction = 2 / (6 − 1) = 0.4
    corrected overlap = (raw overlap − chance fraction) / (1 − chance fraction)
    = (0.5 − 0.4) / (1 − 0.4)
    = 0.1 / 0.6 ≈ 0.167

    The corrected value is near zero when agreement is at the chance baseline, and one when neighbor sets agree perfectly. It can be negative if overlap is below the baseline. It is not a percentage of scientific facts shared between models.

    The paper-linked guide reports k = 20 as the main setting, with sensitivity checks at 5, 10, 50 and 100. Smaller k emphasizes the closest local relationships; larger k includes a broader neighborhood. Changing k changes the question, so report it with the score.

    Because the final comparison uses object IDs, one model can have 768 features and another 1,024. Their numerical coordinates do not need to line up for neighbor overlap to be computed. Their rows and identities do.

    Remember

    Cosine produces within-view similarities. Neighbor selection turns them into lists of object IDs. MKNN compares those lists across views.

    Note 06

    Why inspect intermediate stages and block updates?

    Question: Why not measure only the final output of each model?

    A model repeatedly transforms its input. A block is an architecture-defined unit of that computation. A saved representation stage can be a block output, an input embedding, a normalization output, a pooled representation or a final projection. “Stage” is therefore more precise than calling every saved array a layer.

    Early and late representations may preserve different information. A final output can specialize toward the task used during pretraining. It is a hypothesis—not a rule—that later stages should better capture the scientific relationships of interest.

    A forward hook is a function called when a model component finishes its computation. It lets the extraction program save an intermediate output without changing the model’s weights. A model adapter provides a consistent interface around different architectures.

    1. Load the checkpoint and its expected preprocessing.
    2. Select the components to observe and attach hooks.
    3. Run a batch in evaluation mode without gradient recording.
    4. Convert valid outputs to one row per input object using a documented pooling rule.
    5. Save stage names, shapes, object IDs and values.
    6. Remove the hooks, including when an error interrupts the run.

    Evaluation mode changes behaviors such as dropout to their inference setting. No-gradient execution avoids retaining information needed to update model weights. They solve different problems and are commonly used together.

    State versus change

    A saved state is the current representation. A block update is the difference between compatible consecutive states. If one state is [2, 5] and the next is [3, 4], the net change is [1, −1].

    ΔZ = Z_next − Z_current

    Z is a matrix with one row per galaxy. The Greek delta, Δ, means change. Subtraction is valid only if the rows, widths and architectural interpretations are compatible. It measures the net difference, which can include normalization effects; it does not automatically isolate one causal mechanism.

    The study compares the geometry of these updates as well as the states. An aligned update can occur before the accumulated state reaches maximum alignment. Observing that timing suggests further experiments; it does not prove that changing a specific feature would change the scientific prediction.

    Note 07

    Pairing the data correctly and preventing leakage

    Question: What can invalidate the experiment before a metric is even calculated?

    A row mismatch. If optical row 17 describes galaxy A while infrared row 17 describes galaxy B, the program can still multiply arrays and output plausible scores. It is answering the wrong paired question. Stable object identifiers and explicit joins are essential.

    Paired product in the original study notesObjectsRole
    HSC–DESI20,321Image–spectrum comparison and learned common-space analysis.
    HSC–Legacy Survey101,619Depth analysis and physical transfer.
    HSC–JWST929Optical–infrared depth and transfer analysis.
    HSC–COSMOS-Web layerwise product56,322Intermediate-stage geometry.
    HSC–COSMOS-Web terminal product45,000Separate final-stage physical-transfer experiment.

    The two COSMOS-Web products are not interchangeable. Equal row numbers across different files are not a join key. The original notes also describe removing all occurrences of duplicated IDs and excluding invalid rows. SDSS was excluded from the analyzed basket because of the stated representation-domain issue; it should not quietly be counted in the retained results.

    Different data groups have different jobs

    A training or fit set estimates model settings. A validation set chooses options such as which stage or regularization value to use. A test set measures the final choice on previously unused objects.

    Leakage occurs when information meant to be withheld influences fitting or selection. If you choose a stage using final test performance, the test is no longer independent. Both views of a galaxy must remain in the same role so one physical object does not leak between groups.

    The study notes describe approximately 60% fit, 10% geometry selection, 10% label selection and 20% final test in the smaller transfer experiments, repeated across five folds. Geometry selection uses relationships between views; label selection uses the physical quantity. Keeping their selection groups separate lets the study compare two genuinely different selection methods.

    For the separate terminal COSMOS-Web analysis, spatial slabs and buffer regions reduce leakage between nearby sky areas. Spatial splitting addresses dependence that a simple random shuffle may leave behind.

    Remember

    Matching IDs makes the comparison about the same galaxies. Disjoint roles make its evaluation honest. Correct array dimensions alone establish neither.

    Note 08

    Other geometry metrics: CKA, Procrustes and exact-object retrieval

    Question: Why can one similarity score improve while another gets worse?

    Because they ask different questions. MKNN checks local neighbor membership. Other measures summarize broader shape or test whether an exact counterpart can be retrieved.

    CKA: comparing broad patterns

    Centered kernel alignment (CKA) compares similarity structure across representations. For linear CKA, first center each feature by subtracting its mean across objects. Then compare how the two centered representations vary together relative to their own variation.

    The feature-space formula is useful because it can avoid constructing a huge object-by-object table:

    CKA(X,Y) = ‖XᵀY‖²_F / (‖XᵀX‖_F × ‖YᵀY‖_F)

    X and Y are centered matrices with the same object rows. The superscript T means transpose: swap rows and columns. Matrix multiplication sums products across corresponding entries. ‖M‖_F, the Frobenius norm, means square every entry of matrix M, add them, then take the square root. The numerator squares that norm.

    Linear CKA is unchanged by certain transformations, including orthogonal rotations and uniform scaling. Consequently, a high score does not show that the raw coordinate axes are interchangeable.

    Procrustes: can a rotation align the shapes?

    Procrustes alignment finds a transformation that makes two point collections resemble each other under the allowed constraints. The study’s normalized orthogonal comparison is about rotation-like alignment, including reflection, rather than arbitrary bending.

    If two maps have the same shape but one is rotated, this comparison can be high. A predictor that still reads the old horizontal coordinate may nevertheless fail on the rotated map. The alignment metric explicitly permits changes that the unchanged predictor does not.

    MRR: find this exact galaxy

    Mean reciprocal rank (MRR) asks where the correct paired object appears in a retrieval list. If three queries find their true counterparts at ranks 1, 2 and 5, their reciprocal ranks are 1, 0.5 and 0.2. The mean is (1 + 0.5 + 0.2) / 3 ≈ 0.567.

    Bidirectional retrieval evaluates both directions, such as optical-to-infrared and infrared-to-optical. Direct cross-view scoring requires compatible representations or an explicitly defined mapping. Neighbor overlap does not impose that same coordinate requirement because it compares IDs after within-view search.

    MetricQuestionWhat it does not establish
    MKNNAre the local neighbors the same objects?Identical physical readout directions.
    CKAAre broad centered similarity patterns related?Identical nearest-neighbor lists.
    ProcrustesCan allowed coordinate alignment match the shapes?That no coordinate adjustment is needed.
    MRRHow early is the exact counterpart retrieved?Successful prediction of mass or redshift.
    Remember

    “Better alignment” is incomplete. Name the metric, the stage, the paired data and the transformation allowed by the comparison.

    Note 09

    The physical test: fit one predictor and transfer it unchanged

    Question: Does similar geometry preserve the numerical meaning needed by a scientific predictor?

    A linear probe is a small predictor trained on fixed embeddings. It estimates a physical quantity using a weighted sum of their entries. The foundation model stays frozen; only the probe’s weights are fitted.

    predicted value = b + w₁z₁ + w₂z₂ + … + w_dz_d

    z₁ through z_d are one galaxy’s feature values. d is the number of features. Each w says how strongly its feature contributes, and b is an offset. The dots indicate continuing the sum through all features.

    Ridge regression fits this predictor while penalizing excessively large weights. The penalty can make fitting more stable when features are correlated. Its strength is chosen using validation data, not the final test.

    Standardization subtracts a feature’s fitted mean and divides by its fitted scale. In the strict transfer test, source-view standardization and predictor coefficients remain unchanged on the other survey. Refitting them on the target would change the question.

    A rotation shows the problem exactly

    Illustration — a deliberately simple coordinate change

    In view A, a galaxy is represented by [2, 1]. Suppose the target is the first coordinate, so the predictor returns 2. Rotate every vector by 90 degrees: this galaxy becomes [−1, 2] in view B.

    The rotation preserves all within-view angles and distances, so neighbor relationships can stay identical. But the unchanged predictor still reads the first coordinate and now returns −1 instead of 2. Reading the second coordinate would solve this toy case, but that would be an adapted predictor.

    The example is not a claim that actual telescope shift is just a rotation. It demonstrates logically why preserving geometry alone cannot guarantee unchanged coefficients will work.

    R²: compare errors with a mean baseline

    R² measures squared prediction errors relative to predicting the test targets’ mean. Squaring makes larger errors count more and prevents positive and negative errors from canceling.

    Illustrative calculation

    True values: [1, 2, 3]. Their mean is 2. Predicting [2, 2, 2] gives squared errors 1 + 0 + 1 = 2.

    A transferred predictor gives [3, 3, 3]. Its squared errors are 4 + 1 + 0 = 5. Therefore R² = 1 − 5/2 = −1.5.

    R² = 1 − Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² / Σ(yᵢ − ȳ_test)²

    yᵢ is the true target for test object i; ŷᵢ is its prediction; ȳ_test is the mean true target in the test set; and Σ means add across test objects. A score of 1 is perfect, 0 matches that baseline error, and a negative value is worse. Constant test targets need special handling because the denominator is zero.

    Negative R² is not negative accuracy and does not mean the physical quantity itself is negative. It also does not prove that all useful target information is absent from the embeddings.

    Remember

    The strict test freezes the coordinate-reading rule. A successful target-domain refit or nonlinear predictor would be useful, but would answer a different question.

    Note 10

    Statistics: finding a good stage without fooling yourself

    Question: If we inspect many stages, how do we know the best score is not just lucky?

    Choosing the maximum gives chance many opportunities. Looking at 40 stages and reporting only the highest alignment is a search, so the chance comparison must repeat that search.

    A permutation test creates a reference world by shuffling which objects correspond across surveys. This breaks the true matching while retaining the available representations. For each shuffle, recompute the stage scores and retain their maximum. The observed maximum is compared with these shuffled maxima.

    Observed result:
  calculate every stage → save the largest score

Each shuffled trial:
  shuffle cross-survey correspondence
  calculate every stage using that same shuffle
  save the largest score

Compare observed maximum with the shuffled maxima.

    A p-value summarizes how often the null procedure produces a result at least as extreme as the observed one. It is not the probability that the scientific hypothesis is true. For a finite permutation sample, a common calculation includes the observed case:

    p = (1 + number of null maxima at least as large as observed) / (1 + number of permutations)

    The original study notes report 9,999 permutations per depth curve. They also report Holm correction across the 175 originally evaluated curves. Holm’s procedure orders p-values and adjusts the rejection thresholds to control false positives across the family of tests. The 139 retained non-SDSS comparisons are not the same count as the original correction family.

    Uncertainty is a different question from chance significance

    A bootstrap repeatedly samples observations with replacement and recomputes the result. “With replacement” means an object can appear several times in one resample. The variation across resamples estimates sensitivity to which objects were observed.

    Paired views must be resampled together. When evaluating an entire depth curve, use the same sampled object IDs across stages in a draw so their dependence is preserved. The guide reports 2,000 paired-object draws for curve uncertainty.

    Several checkpoints from one model family are also related. For conclusions across architectures, family-level resampling treats an entire family as the unit rather than pretending every variant is an independent discovery.

    Remember

    Permutation asks whether true pairing gives more agreement than the null search. Bootstrap asks how much the estimate varies with the sampled objects or families. Neither proves a causal mechanism or successful physics transfer.

    Note 11

    Learning a common space: PCA, CCA and controls

    Question: Can we learn a conversion between the views?

    Yes, that is a useful additional experiment. But it must be described as adaptation. The strict unchanged-transfer test asks whether the original representations already share a usable coordinate reading; a learned mapping adds a new fitted component.

    Principal component analysis (PCA) finds directions along which the feature values vary most. Keeping only the leading directions compresses the vectors. High variance is not automatically scientific relevance: a dominant direction could reflect brightness, instrument differences or another nuisance.

    Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) learns a projection for each of two matched views so their projected values correlate. It can use paired objects without using physical labels such as redshift. “No physical labels” still does not mean “no fitting”: object correspondence teaches the mapping.

    The original notes describe a rank-64 PCA–regularized CCA experiment for HSC images and DESI spectra. Rank 64 means using a 64-dimensional common representation. Its reported held-out mean MRR is 0.038. That is an exact-object retrieval result after adaptation, not an R² score and not proof of unchanged physical transfer.

    Fit preprocessing and mapping only on training objects. Applying a transformation to test rows is fine; learning the transformation from those rows before claiming an untouched test is not.

    What controls can and cannot tell us

    A control changes part of the experiment to test an alternative explanation. Shuffling object pairing checks whether identity correspondence matters. Independent random Gaussian features test whether array shape alone produces similar apparent agreement. Random projections test sensitivity to dimension reduction.

    An ablation removes part of a representation or method. Removing leading principal components can reduce broad global agreement while improving local neighbor overlap. That is possible because global and local metrics emphasize different relationships.

    The original guide reports approximately 71% retained local alignment after random projection to 64 dimensions and approximately 128% after removing five leading components. These are relative to the stated baseline, not accuracy percentages. Regressing out a few known catalog properties cannot rule out every possible survey-related confounder.

    Remember

    A learned common space can improve retrieval while leaving the original no-adaptation result intact. Controls narrow explanations; they do not automatically identify the cause.

    Note 12

    The engineering pipeline: extract once, analyze many times

    Question: How do researchers run all these comparisons without repeating every expensive model call?

    Separate representation extraction from analysis. Once the frozen model’s outputs are saved with reliable metadata, many metric settings and probe experiments can reuse them.

    Matched observations and stable IDs
  → preprocessing and batches
  → frozen model + intermediate-output hooks
  → one vector per object per saved stage
  → durable feature files
  → geometry, statistical tests and probes
  → small result tables and figures

    A CPU handles general program work, file reading and preparation. A GPU accelerates many numerical operations at once. System RAM is general working memory; GPU VRAM is the GPU’s working memory. Disk stores durable files. Increasing one does not automatically solve a shortage in another.

    A batch is a group of examples processed together. Larger batches can improve hardware utilization, but also require more memory. The total memory includes model weights, temporary calculations, saved activations and buffers—not only the input images.

    Parquet is a column-oriented file format. Storing one stage’s vectors in a column lets analysis read that stage without necessarily loading every other stage. A cache keeps recently needed columns in memory. An LRU, or least-recently-used, policy evicts columns that have not been used recently when a memory budget is reached.

    Downloading a large artifact once to local scratch storage avoids repeatedly fetching remote pieces. Scratch is temporary workspace; completed results must be written to durable storage before it is cleaned up.

    What must accompany an embedding file?

    • Unique object IDs and exact row order.
    • Source dataset and revision, plus filtering rules.
    • Model checkpoint, preprocessing and code revision.
    • Saved stage names, widths, pooling rule and numerical precision.
    • Split IDs, random seeds and later metric settings.

    A file containing numbers without those details may be impossible to compare or reproduce honestly. A missing stage should be reported as unavailable, not silently assigned zero alignment.

    Remember

    Caching makes work reusable only when the cached artifact’s identity and meaning are known. Reusing the wrong stage or row order efficiently still produces the wrong experiment.

    Note 13

    GPU memory: why chunking helps, and what it does not fix

    Question: Why can comparing vectors require more memory than storing them?

    Suppose there are 100,000 galaxies. Comparing every galaxy with every other creates 100,000 × 100,000 = 10 billion scores. A float32 number occupies four bytes, so that full table requires about 40 billion bytes, or 40 decimal GB, before any other arrays are counted.

    Instead, process 4,096 query rows at a time against the full reference set. This score chunk needs 4096 × 100000 × 4 bytes, about 1.64 GB. For each row, retain only the best 20 neighbor IDs, discard the score chunk and move to the next rows.

    If neighbor IDs are 64-bit integers, each uses eight bytes. The retained table needs 100000 × 20 × 8 bytes, or 16 MB. That is much smaller than keeping all pairwise scores. Additional top-k values, masks, embeddings and temporary arrays still consume memory.

    Memory calculator

    Decimal GB/MB; float32 scores and int64 IDs. Estimates only these arrays, not the full workload.

    Lower peak memory is not less total pairwise work

    Chunking still compares all pairs if every chunk is processed exhaustively. Ten times as many objects gives approximately 100 times as many pairs. This is quadratic growth. Chunking manages where intermediate results live; it does not make exact search subquadratic.

    FAISS is a vector-search library with both exact and approximate options. An exact flat inner-product index can compute cosine neighbors when vectors are normalized. Merely saying “FAISS” does not imply approximate search. Approximate methods should be benchmarked for missed neighbors and any resulting bias in the scientific metric.

    CKA’s feature-space formula can avoid an object-by-object matrix, but its products grow with feature width. Extremely wide output heads can still be expensive. The original notes distinguish a worker width limit from paper-specific reductions that preserve the relevant row geometry; those are not the same implementation choice.

    Remember

    Count the largest intermediate, not just the saved output. State whether an optimization saves peak memory, arithmetic, downloads or repeated model inference.

    Note 14

    SLURM, GPU workers and recovery from failure

    Question: How do many independent research tasks get assigned to available hardware?

    SLURM is a cluster scheduler. A job script requests resources such as GPUs, CPU cores, RAM and maximum runtime. The scheduler queues the job until resources are available, then starts the program. A worker is the running process that handles tasks.

    A task might be one model–survey extraction or a range of stage comparisons. Several workers can process different tasks at once. This is task parallelism. Splitting one model’s weights or layers across GPUs is model parallelism, which requires different coordination. Seeing eight GPUs in a resource description does not establish that a model was sharded across all eight.

    The original paper-linked notes report an eight-A100 environment, high-memory CPU resources and about 1,500 project-wide GPU-hours. One GPU running for an hour contributes one GPU-hour; eight GPUs running for one hour contribute eight. GPU-hours are not the same as elapsed wall-clock time or an individual contributor’s workload.

    The repository’s example solve wrapper requests one GPU, eight CPU cores, 512 GB of host RAM and a 24-hour time limit. Those are example worker requests, not proof that every experiment used that exact allocation.

    The task lifecycle

    Pending task
  → claim it
  → load required artifacts
  → compute and validate
  → write a temporary result
  → publish the completed result
  → mark done and release the claim

    A lock prevents competing workers from taking conflicting actions. A local create-if-absent lock file can be atomic for processes sharing the filesystem. The documented remote running/done markers use a less strict pattern: two workers can both observe that no marker exists before either uploads its claim.

    This race can duplicate work. Stale markers from crashed jobs also need recovery. The notes describe reclaiming old markers and making tasks restartable, but not a globally exactly-once transaction.

    Idempotent output means repeating a logical task leaves the intended result unchanged. Stable task IDs, checksums and immutable outputs help detect duplicates. A temporary-file-then-rename pattern prevents a partially written local result from being mistaken for a completed one, within the filesystem guarantees being used.

    SymptomFirst useful check
    GPU out of memoryBatch size, stage width, chunk size and retained tensors.
    GPU mostly idleTime spent downloading, decoding files and preparing input.
    Duplicate resultsTask IDs, claim timing and competing attempt records.
    Changing similarity on repeated runsRow order, preprocessing, precision, tie handling and deterministic settings.
    Remember

    More workers improve throughput only if tasks are independent enough and storage, memory and coordination can support them. Practical recovery is not the same as a strict exactly-once guarantee.

    Note 16

    Reproducibility, personal contributions and interpretability

    Question: Which claims belong to the paper, which to the code, and which need separate evidence?

    A paper reports a group study. Repository files show implementation and documented contribution scope. A personal performance claim needs its own experiment record. These sources can support each other, but they should not be merged into a larger claim than any of them establishes.

    The checked contribution document attributes modality-adapter work to Aravind Kannappan. It describes time-series, spectrum and tabular interfaces; real-weight checks for several models; stub-only or gated/dependency-limited cases for others; and remaining astronomical dataset wiring.

    A stub test uses a small substitute to check interface behavior without downloading the real model. A real-weight smoke test checks that the actual model can run on a small input. Neither alone is a complete scientific benchmark on real survey data.

    The contribution document also describes repeat-run checks and self-MKNN checks. Identical inputs and a deterministic extraction path should yield consistent outputs; identical neighbor graphs should agree with themselves. Seeds help, but row ordering, library versions, numerical precision and equal-score tie handling can still matter.

    How sparse autoencoders and patching differ from this study’s main comparison

    A sparse autoencoder (SAE) learns to reconstruct activations using an intermediate representation encouraged to have relatively few active features. The hope is that those features expose interpretable patterns. Naming a feature is an interpretation that requires independent checking.

    A crosscoder learns relationships among features from multiple models or stages. Activation patching replaces internal values during a model run and observes the effect on an output. This is an intervention; measuring two naturally occurring representations is an observation.

    If replacing a chosen activation changes a prediction under appropriate controls, that offers stronger causal evidence than correlation alone. Controls must ensure the intervention did not simply corrupt the entire representation. The original guide separates CV-reported SAE and patching claims from the paper’s central geometry and linear-transfer tests.

    Specific historical numbers for probe accuracy, dictionary size, indexed embeddings or speedup require the corresponding datasets, logs, baseline and procedure. They are not independently verified by rewriting this guide.

    Remember

    Explain what you implemented, what you ran, what you measured and what the team concluded as separate statements. This makes an explanation clearer and easier to defend.

    Note 17

    A beginner’s reproduction checklist

    Question: What should be verified before attempting the full experiment?

    Begin with a small run that checks meaning and shape. Scaling a broken pairing or pooling rule only produces more incorrect results.

    1. Pin the inputs. Record the repository revision, checkpoint, dataset revision and preprocessing. Follow the repository’s current environment instructions rather than mixing incompatible historical commands.
    2. Run interface tests. Confirm that an adapter accepts a small batch and returns one row per object at each supported stage.
    3. Check real weights separately. A stub test cannot establish that external weights download or execute correctly. Some models require access permission or an isolated dependency environment.
    4. Verify object matching. Assert unique IDs, identical paired order, finite values and expected row counts.
    5. Extract a small artifact. Save vectors and metadata. Reload it and confirm values, stage names and IDs survive.
    6. Check a metric manually. On a tiny dataset, compare computed neighbor IDs and overlap with a hand calculation. Exclude self-neighbors and define tie handling.
    7. Run sanity controls. Compare a representation with itself and then break correspondence deliberately. These catch different classes of errors.
    8. Freeze selection roles. Fit preprocessing only on permitted rows, choose settings on validation roles and preserve the final test.
    9. Measure resources. Record memory and time before increasing the batch, object count or model width.
    10. Scale with restartable tasks. Keep scratch separate from durable results and record each task’s attempt and completion state.

    The repository includes extraction, computation and regression workflows. The original guide warns that its quickstart and some repeated 80/20 ordinary-least-squares scripts are not automatically the same as the paper’s ridge and disjoint-selection protocol. Matching a command name is not sufficient to reproduce a published number.

    Ordinary least squares fits a linear predictor without the ridge penalty described earlier. Changing the fitting method, splits or normalization can change results, so those details belong in the reproduction record.

    Note 18

    Revision notes and questions to answer aloud

    Image → tokens → features
    A model applies learned calculations to measurements. Pooling gives a vector per galaxy. The entries are not automatically named physical quantities.
    Cosine → neighbors → overlap
    Cosine compares within-view feature patterns. Neighbor lists identify similar objects. MKNN compares those identities between views.
    Geometry → prediction is an extra test
    Preserved relationships can coexist with changed coordinate meanings. Test the same predictor rather than assuming transfer.
    Selection → evaluation
    Choosing a stage is part of the procedure. Validation roles and the permutation null must account for that choice.
    Memory → computation
    Chunking bounds temporary storage while exact pairwise work remains quadratic.
    Why do [1,2,3] and [2,4,6] have cosine similarity 1?

    The second is twice the first. Normalizing each removes that length difference and leaves identical relative entries. This says nothing by itself about whether the corresponding galaxies have equal physical properties.

    Can two spaces have identical neighbors and incompatible physical readouts?

    Yes. Rotate one space. Within-space relationships are preserved, but a predictor reading the old coordinate can now read the wrong one. A learned correction would be adaptation.

    What does a transferred R² of −1.5 mean?

    The squared prediction error is 2.5 times the test-mean baseline error, because 1 − 2.5 = −1.5. It does not mean negative accuracy or absence of all useful information.

    Why compare the observed best stage with shuffled best stages?

    Both procedures must receive the same opportunity to find a lucky maximum. A single-stage null would ignore the search that produced the observed best result.

    Why can a 40 GB similarity table become a 1.64 GB chunk without making exact search fast at every scale?

    Only 4,096 query rows are stored at once, but all query rows are eventually processed. Peak memory falls; total pairwise comparisons remain approximately proportional to the square of the object count.

    What is the careful research conclusion?

    “The tested models often organized matched galaxies similarly across surveys, but that resemblance did not guarantee that an unchanged linear predictor could recover the tested physical quantities. The result leaves room for adaptation and other predictors.”
